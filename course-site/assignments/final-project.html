Final Project — Certify the Controller, Then Defend It

Design a controller for your own simulated quadrotor, submit it for certification against a written specification, and defend your understanding of it 1-on-1.
⚖️ Weight: 40% of the course 🧍 Individual — all 100 marks, no team block 🆔 Your own vehicle: --student <your ID> 📄 Report ≤6 pages + individual viva, 5 min hard cut (Week 15)

← Team case study brief (Week 10) All assignments Download PDF

Task This project is individual. One mission, the same for everyone: M3, the payload drop — but every student certifies a different aircraft. Certify your Week-9 autopilot against it and watch it fail exactly one requirement. Upgrade it with MPC (Week 12). Certify again. Then explain why the number moved. Your controller is tested by the same automated script, run once, the same way for every student, against a written specification — not a live demo. See simulator/CERTIFICATION.md for what certification means as an engineering practice and why the project is graded this way.

Your own aircraft — --student <your ID>

The final project is individual, and so is the vehicle. Passing --student <your ID> makes the harness build your quadcopter before it flies the mission: your mass (and your mass step), your arm length, your yaw-torque ratio, your inertia — all derived from your student ID. See your own numbers with:

python -m quadsim.student_params <your ID>

The derivation is deterministic, not random: the same ID always produces the same aircraft, on any machine, forever — so your result reproduces exactly, and a different ID never produces yours. The requirements do not change: same three IDs, same 0.50 m bars, same graded seed, for everyone.

What this changes, and what it does not Still fine, still encouraged: discuss the theory, argue about horizons and cost weights, read each other's code, help each other past an import error. What no longer works: copying a tuned controller — gains that close REQ-M3-1 on one vehicle are not guaranteed to close it on another. Record your student ID in your README: a run with the wrong ID certifies an aircraft that is not yours and does not count.

The mission — M3, payload drop

The quadrotor holds position at [0, 0, 1.5]. At t = 5 s its mass steps down — a package released (0.65 kg → 0.45 kg on the course default vehicle; your own two masses come from your ID). Your controller is never told. It has one continuous instance for the whole 15 s flight and has to notice and correct through feedback alone, exactly as a real flight computer would. Three requirements:

IDRequirementThreshold
REQ-M3-1Max deviation across the entire post-drop window< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-2Max transient deviation in the first 3 s after the drop< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-3Position RMSE over the last 5 s of the run< 0.50 m

Graded on estimator seed 0 — one command shape, the same fixed draw for everyone, with your own ID:

python certify.py --controller <your.module:YourClass> --student <your ID> --mission M3

--seeds 0,1,2 exists to measure the spread for the one required sentence in your report. It is not the grading rule: the grade comes from seed 0.

Here is the whole project in one before-and-after, using the course's own reference controllers on the course default vehicle (run with no --student flag) — the shared worked example used in lectures. Your own numbers will differ, because your aircraft differs; the pattern holds for everyone (verified by sweeping 200 synthetic student IDs): the classical baseline fails exactly one requirement, and the MPC upgrade fixes that one.

M1 and M2 are demonstration missions only: their bars are not achievable by any controller, which is itself the Week-11 requirements-review exercise. Full detail in simulator/CERTIFICATION.md §2.

What you hand in — three things

  1. The two certification reports, pasted verbatim — your frozen Week-12 baseline and your final submission, both run with --student <your ID>. Not paraphrased, not retyped.
  2. What you changed, written as engineering — the MPC formulation in plain terms with its key equations: prediction model, horizon, what is in the cost, what you constrained and whether it was hard or soft. Every symbol one you chose and can defend.
  3. One figure and one paragraph explaining the mechanism — a plot showing why the number moved (the altitude trace through the mass drop is usually clearest), and a paragraph naming the specific feature of MPC responsible.

Plus one honest sentence on the across-seed spread (run --seeds 0,1,2 once — it measures spread, it does not change the grade) and one paragraph on what you would do next with another month. Six pages is plenty.

Optional stretch A constraint-activation plot, a solve-time histogram against the deadline, or an ablation table (shorter horizon / hard-only constraints / no Δu term). Not required, and not needed for full marks — attempt these only once the three required items are genuinely finished.

Milestones

  • Week 11 — one-page proposal per person, justified by your own baseline certification numbers on your own vehicle: your student ID and vehicle parameters, which requirement you are attacking, your approach, your fallback
  • Week 12 — baseline frozen (metrics script + student ID + seeds committed); post-freeze changes need sign-off
  • Week 13 — method running on your vehicle + first evidence figure
  • Week 14 — supervised clinic: results complete and a full certification dry run passed (fresh clone, tag final, network off)
  • Before the Week 15 session — code + report + engineering log submitted, both certification reports pasted in
  • Week 15 session — individual viva only, 5 minutes hard cut per person — no presentation, no live demo; the team case study was Week 10. Full brief, marking anchors and the question bank: Week 15 viva brief

Rules

  • This is individual work. Discuss the theory freely, read each other's code, help each other debug — but the controller you submit, the report, the log and the viva answers must be yours. Because every student's vehicle differs, borrowed tuning does not transfer anyway.
  • Your student ID is part of the submission. Put it in your README. A run with the wrong ID certifies a vehicle that is not yours and does not count.
  • Certification runs once, by the instructor, from a fresh clone of your repo at tag final, with --student <your ID> and the network off — the same procedure for every student. Run it yourself first; the instructor's run is a confirmation, not a surprise.
  • The viva timer is a hard cut — it ends at 5:00 regardless of where the conversation is.
  • AI & code provenance: use whatever tools you like, declare what you used and for what, and be ready to explain every line you submit at the viva. Unexplainable code is not your evidence.
  • Late code/report: −20% per day; nothing accepted after the Week 15 session (the viva schedule cannot be made up except for institutional cases).
