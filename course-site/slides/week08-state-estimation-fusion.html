First principles

Estimation is inference, not measurement

  • No sensor measures 'attitude'. A gyro measures rotation rate; an accelerometer measures specific force. Attitude is inferred.
  • No indoor sensor measures position. Optical flow measures ground-relative velocity in pixels; a ToF laser measures distance to whatever is below. Position is inferred, and it drifts.
  • So the estimator's job is: given a model of how the world evolves and a stream of partial, noisy, delayed clues, maintain the best current belief about twelve numbers nobody can see.
The reframing that matters. Weeks 1–7 treated \( \mathbf x \) as a fact. From today it is a hypothesis with an uncertainty attached.

That change propagates: your controller now acts on a belief, your safety logic must handle low-confidence beliefs, and 'the aircraft knows where it is' becomes a claim requiring evidence.

This is the week the course stops being about an aircraft and starts being about an aircraft that has to figure things out.

Set the philosophical frame early; it makes the covariance discussion later feel necessary rather than decorative.

Detail, done properly

Noise, bias, drift: three different problems

NoiseBiasDrift
Looks likefast, zero-mean scattera constant offseta slow wander

Gyro0.01 rad/s RMS0.5–3 °/stemperature-dependent
Effect if ignoredjittery controlunbounded attitude errorslow, plausible lies
Why bias is the dangerous one. Noise averages away — it is annoying, not fatal. Bias does not average away, and integration turns it into an error that grows without limit. A 1 °/s bias is 60° of attitude error after one minute.

That is why every serious estimator either calibrates bias on the ground or carries it as a state and learns it in flight. Your aircraft does the first every time you arm it while holding still.
(90 s) — Your team's gyro sits at 1.2 °/s bias. How long before attitude-from-gyro-alone is 10° wrong? (8.3 seconds. That is the entire useful life of an uncorrected gyro attitude — and the reason the accelerometer, however noisy, is not optional.)
The 8-second number is startling and makes the case for fusion better than any argument.

Mental picture first

Two blurry photographs of the same thing

  • Suppose two people estimate a height: one says \( 2.0 \pm 0.2 \) m, the other \( 2.3 \pm 0.1 \) m. What is the best single answer?
  • Not the average (2.15). Not the more confident one alone (2.3). The right answer is \( 2.24 \) m — and, remarkably, it is sharper than either estimate: \( \pm 0.089 \) m.
  • Two imperfect views, combined by confidence, beat both. That single idea is the Kalman filter; the matrices are bookkeeping for doing it with twelve numbers at 200 Hz.
(2 min) — Before we derive anything: why is the fused answer sharper than both inputs? (Because the errors are independent. Two independent clues about the same fact carry more information than either alone — precision adds. If the errors were correlated, this would not hold, and assuming independence when it is false is how filters quietly go wrong.)
Lead with the numeric puzzle before any formalism; the 'sharper than both' surprise is what motivates the whole week.

Detail, done properly

Initialisation: the first second decides the next minute

  • A filter needs \( \hat{\mathbf x}_0 \) and \( P_0 \). Getting \( P_0 \) wrong is the subtler error.
  • Too small \( P_0 \): the filter is certain it is at the origin. Measurements are treated as outliers, and it can take many seconds to admit the truth — or never, if gating is on.
  • Too large \( P_0 \): it snaps to the first measurement, noise and all — usually harmless, and the safer error.
What arming actually does. Hold still, and the aircraft: levels attitude from the accelerometer, zeros the gyro bias by averaging, sets \( z = 0 \) at the ToF reading, and sets \( P_0 \) generously.

That is why 'do not move it while arming' is a real instruction, not superstition — movement corrupts the bias average, and a corrupted bias is the 8-seconds-to-10-degrees problem from earlier.

Your Week-10 checklist has 'hold still during arming' as a line item. You now know precisely which estimate it protects.

Explaining a procedure the students already follow makes the checklist feel earned rather than bureaucratic.

Concept check

Two questions before the lab

Q1 — Your filter reports σ = 2 cm on position, but comparing with truth shows 15 cm of error. Which is wrong, and what do you change?

The filter is overconfident — its uncertainty model is wrong, not its estimate. Raise \( Q \) (admit the model is worse than assumed) or \( R \) (admit the sensor is worse). An overconfident filter is dangerous because downstream logic — outlier gating, safety checks — trusts that σ.

Q2 — Would running the estimator at 400 Hz instead of 200 Hz improve position accuracy?

Almost not at all. Accuracy is set by the measurement rate and quality (flow at 40 Hz), not by how often you predict between measurements. Faster prediction reduces discretisation error only — a second-order effect. Spend the compute on latency compensation instead.

Q2 corrects a common instinct: more Hz is not more information.

Block A

Dead reckoning and its debt

Attitude from the gyro alone: integrate.

\[ \hat\theta(t) = \int_0^t \big(\omega_{\text{true}} + b + n(t)\big)\,d\tau = \theta_{\text{true}} + \underbrace{b\,t}_{\text{grows forever}} + \text{noise walk} \]
  • A modest 1°/s bias ⇒ 60° wrong in one minute. Uncorrected, unflyable.
  • The accelerometer knows which way is down — bias-free, but shaky and fooled by acceleration.
The pattern to memorize:
integrate a relative sensor → smooth but drifting;
an absolute sensor → noisy but anchored.

Every fusion problem in robotics is these two characters, forced to cooperate.

Block A

The complementary filter: one line of cooperation

\[ \hat\theta_{k+1} = \underbrace{\alpha\,\big(\hat\theta_k + \omega_k\,\Delta t\big)}_{\text{trust the gyro's motion}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,\theta_k^{\text{acc}}}_{\text{let gravity vote}} \]
  • High-pass the gyro path, low-pass the accel path — they complement: their transfer functions sum to 1.
  • Crossover \( \tau \approx \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Delta t \): with \( \alpha=0.98 \) at 50 Hz, ~1 s — faster than bias drift, slower than vibration.
  • Three lines of C. Runs on anything. This is what most hobby flight controllers actually ship.
Widget: crank gyro bias to 3°/s — amber line walks away, teal stays. Then α→0.999: watch fused inherit the drift (gyro trusted too long). The knob IS the tradeoff.

Derivation · click to advance interactive

The complementary filter, derived rather than asserted

1gyro path: \( \hat\theta_g(s) = \theta(s) + \dfrac{b}{s} \) — truth plus an integrated bias Good at high frequency, wrong at low. Integration means the bias enters as \( 1/s \): unbounded at DC.
2accel path: \( \hat\theta_a(s) = \theta(s) + n(s) \) — truth plus wideband noise Good at low frequency, noisy at high. No drift, because gravity is an absolute reference.
3combine with complementary weights: \( \hat\theta = \underbrace{\tfrac{\tau s}{\tau s+1}}_{\text{high-pass}}\hat\theta_g + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\tau s+1}}_{\text{low-pass}}\hat\theta_a The two filters sum to exactly 1 at every frequency — that is what 'complementary' means. So the true signal passes through untouched, while each sensor is used only where it is trustworthy.
4discretise: \( \hat\theta_{k+1} = \alpha(\hat\theta_k + \omega_k\Delta t) + (1-\alpha)\theta^{acc}_k \), \( \alpha = \dfrac{\tau}{\tau + \Delta t} \) Three lines of C. \( \alpha = 0.98 \) at 50 Hz ⇒ \( \tau = 0.98 \) s: trust the gyro for about a second, then let gravity have its say.

Choosing \( \tau \) is choosing a crossover: faster than the bias grows, slower than the vehicle accelerates. On a quadrotor, roughly 0.5–2 s. Everything else is arithmetic.

Deriving the filter in the frequency domain explains WHY it works, which the usual alpha-blend presentation hides.

Sensor rates, latency, and the timestamp you must keep

SensorRateLatencyConsequence

Latency is not noise. A delayed measurement is not a noisy one — it is a correct measurement of the past. Fusing it as if it were current injects a systematic error proportional to velocity.

The professional cure: timestamp every measurement at capture, and fuse it against the state estimate as it was at that instant (a short state buffer). PX4 and ArduPilot both do this. It is why serious estimators keep history.
(90 s) — At 1 m/s with 40 ms of flow latency, how large is the position error if you ignore the delay? (4 cm at 40 ms — and the Week-10 plant delays GPS by 120–300 ms, so 12–30 cm at the same speed. Latency is not a detail at this scale; it is most of the error budget.)
The 4 cm calculation makes latency concrete; scale it to the team plant's 120–300 ms and the Week-10 hunting is predicted in advance.

Detail, done properly

Tuning \( Q \) and \( R \) — the filter's only real knobs

  • \( R \) is measurable. Sit the aircraft still, record 60 s, compute the variance. That is your \( R \) — no guessing, and it is a Role-4 milestone.
  • \( Q \) is a modelling statement: how much do I distrust my own propagation? Larger \( Q \) ⇒ the filter admits the model is wrong ⇒ leans on measurements ⇒ responsive but noisy.
  • Tune \( Q \) by watching the innovation \( z - H\hat{\mathbf x}^- \): it should look like white noise with the variance the filter predicts.
The consistency check that separates real work from decoration. Compute the normalised innovation squared. If the filter's claimed uncertainty is honest, this quantity averages to the measurement dimension (1 for a scalar).

>1 ⇒ the filter is overconfident (raise \( Q \) or \( R \)).
<1 ⇒ underconfident, throwing away information.

This is how you know a filter is tuned — not by whether the plot looks smooth.
The NIS consistency check is graduate-level and immediately actionable; it is also the answer to 'how do I know Q is right?'.

Detail, done properly

EKF: what changes when the model is nonlinear

  1. Propagate the mean through the true nonlinear \( f \) — no approximation here.
  2. Propagate covariance through the Jacobian \( F = \partial f/\partial \mathbf x \), evaluated at the current estimate, not at hover.
  3. Update exactly as before, with \( H = \partial h/\partial\mathbf x \).
  • So the EKF re-linearises every step, about wherever it currently believes it is. Week 4's machinery, running at 200 Hz.
  • Cost: the Jacobians. Risk: if the estimate is badly wrong, it linearises about the wrong point and can diverge confidently.
Why it still dominates. Nothing else is as cheap. The alternatives — unscented (sample the distribution), particle (sample everything) — cost 2n+1 or thousands of propagations per step. On a flight controller's budget, the EKF's occasional divergence is a better deal than the UKF's certain expense.

PX4, ArduPilot, and your team's firmware all run an EKF. Now you know both why, and what to watch for.
The 'diverge confidently' failure mode is the one to name; it is what innovation monitoring is defending against.

The man and the prerequisite

One page of probability is all today needs

Kálmán's 1960 filter was dismissed by several journals, adopted by the Apollo program within three years, and now runs in every phone, car and aircraft on Earth. It asks one question, recursively: given everything so far, what do I believe, and how strongly?

The only prerequisite. A Gaussian \( \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) \) is fully described by mean + variance. Two independent Gaussian beliefs about the same quantity multiply into a Gaussian with \[ \sigma^{-2} = \sigma_1^{-2} + \sigma_2^{-2}, \qquad \mu = \sigma^2\big(\tfrac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2} + \tfrac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\big) \] — precision adds; means average, weighted by confidence. The entire Kalman update is this formula wearing matrices.
Worked example (fuse two rulers). Belief A: height \( 2.00 \) m, \( \sigma = 0.20 \). Sensor B: \( 2.30 \) m, \( \sigma = 0.10 \). Precisions: 25 + 100 = 125 ⇒ \( \sigma_{post} = 0.089 \) m, \( \mu_{post} = (25\cdot2.00 + 100\cdot2.30)/125 = 2.24 \) m. The fused answer sits 4× closer to the better sensor and is sharper than either. Do this once by hand and the Kalman gain \( K = P^-/(P^-+R) \) is no longer a formula — it is this example.
The precision-adds identity is the deepest single line of the week. Students compute the 2.24 by hand in 90 seconds — make them, before showing the matrix version.

Block B · 01:00

From blending to belief

  • The complementary filter blends with a fixed knob. But trust should move: mid-dropout, trust the model; right after three crisp measurements, trust the sensor.
  • Upgrade: carry a full belief — mean \( \hat{\mathbf{x}} \) and covariance \(P\) ("how wrong might I be?").
  • Two honest inputs: process noise \(Q\) (how much the world surprises the model), measurement noise \(R\) (how much the sensor lies).
Everything is Gaussian by fiat, and then two rules run forever:

Predict: push belief through the model — uncertainty grows.
Update: collide belief with a measurement — uncertainty shrinks.

The optimal collision ratio is the Kalman gain.

Derivation · click to advance interactive

The Kalman gain is not a decree — derive it in four steps

1blend: \( \hat x = \hat x^- + K\,(z - \hat x^-) \), choose \( K \in [0,1] \) Commit to a linear blend of prior and measurement — the only honest move given two uncertain numbers.
2posterior variance: \( P = (1-K)^2 P^- + K^2 R \) Propagate uncertainty through the blend — independent errors add in quadrature, weighted by their coefficients squared.
3\( \dfrac{dP}{dK} = 0 \Rightarrow K^\ast = \dfrac{P^-}{P^- + R} \) Minimise. One derivative, one line — the "optimal" in optimal filtering is a parabola's vertex, nothing fancier.
4substitute back: \( P = (1-K^\ast)P^- \) Uncertainty shrinks by exactly the trust you placed in the sensor. The matrix equations on the next slide are steps 1–4 with \( H \) bolted on.
(90 s) — plug the two-rulers numbers in: \( P^- = 0.04, R = 0.01 \Rightarrow K = 0.8 \), \( \hat x = 2.0 + 0.8(0.3) = 2.24 \) m. Same answer as the precision formula — as it must be.
Deriving the scalar gain kills the black-box feeling permanently. The matrix version then reads as bookkeeping, which is the correct level of awe.

Block B

The Kalman filter on one slide

Predict (every step) \[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} + B\mathbf{u} \] \[ P^- = APA^\top + Q \] model forward; add humility \(Q\)
Update (when a measurement \( \mathbf{z} \) lands) \[ K = P^-H^\top (HP^-H^\top + R)^{-1} \] \[ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- + K(\mathbf{z} - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-) \] \[ P = (I - KH)P^- \] correct by the surprise, weighted by trust

Read the gain as a fraction: \( K \approx \dfrac{P^-}{P^- + R} \) — my uncertainty over total uncertainty. Sensor dies (\(R\to\infty\)) ⇒ \(K\to 0\): coast on the model, let \(P\) balloon. Perfect sensor ⇒ \(K\to 1\): believe it outright.

Optimal — given linear dynamics, Gaussian noises, correct \(Q,R\). None of those are true on a quad. It works anyway, because the gain-from-uncertainty mechanism is right even when the assumptions wobble. Nonlinear model? Linearize about the current estimate each step: the EKF — which is what real autopilots (PX4, Betaflight, quadsim's estimator) run.

Block B

Feeding the estimate to the controller

  • Swap: \( \mathbf{u} = -K_{\text{lqr}}\hat{\mathbf{x}} \) instead of \( \mathbf{x} \). (LQR + KF = LQG.)
  • Separation principle: for linear-Gaussian systems, designing the controller and estimator independently is exactly optimal. Lovely theorem, real caveat: margins can vanish (LQG has no guaranteed robustness — Doyle, 1978, in one line).
  • Practically: estimator lag acts like sensor delay ⇒ effective phase loss ⇒ back off the gains you tuned on truth.
Rule for Lab 5 and for the aircraft:
estimator bandwidth ≳ 3× the loop that consumes it. Attitude loop at 20 Hz wants attitude estimates good to ~60 Hz — which the IMU can feed. Position from 20 Hz flow feeds a ~2 Hz outer loop. The cascade's timescales (Wk 6) were secretly a sensor-rate budget all along.

One level deeper · the estimator's best trick

Estimate what you cannot measure: the bias state

  • The gyro's slow bias \( b \) poisoned dead reckoning (slide 5). But bias is observable through its consequences: it makes the gyro path disagree with gravity in a consistent direction.
  • So promote it to a state: \( \mathbf x = (\theta, b) \), model \( \dot\theta = \omega_{gyro} - b \), \( \dot b \approx 0 \) (+ small \(Q_b\)) — and let the filter learn the bias online.
  • This is the standard attitude EKF everywhere (PX4, Betaflight, phones): 7 states = quaternion + 3 gyro biases. Your aircraft recalibrating "level" while sitting still is this state converging.
Remark (the pattern generalises). Anything constant-ish that corrupts a measurement can be appended and co-estimated: baro drift, flow-scale error, even battery-sag thrust loss. The price: each new state needs excitation to become observable — a perfectly still quad teaches the filter nothing about flow scale. Observability = "does the data distinguish this state?", and it is checkable (rank test), not vibes.
This is the week's most transferable systems idea. The "recalibrating while still" observation gives students a real artifact they have already seen.

Concept check

Diagnosis drill

Lab-5 symptom: on truth, your quad holds position rock-steady; on the estimate, it orbits the setpoint in a slow ~1 Hz circle. The estimate plots look smooth. Most likely?
A. Estimator noise too high
B. Estimator lag — smooth is not the same as timely
C. The controller is broken

B. Over-smoothed = delayed; delay in a feedback loop = phase loss = the orbit. Raise \(Q\) (or α down): accept some noise, buy back phase. "Smooth" is a seduction — controllers eat fresh, not pretty.

Worked example · by hand

One full Kalman step, on paper

Scalar altitude. Prior: \( \hat z^- = 1.80 \) m, \( P^- = 0.04 \) (σ = 20 cm). ToF reads \( z = 2.05 \) m with \( R = 0.01 \) (σ = 10 cm).

  1. Innovation: \( y = 2.05 - 1.80 = 0.25 \) m.
  2. Innovation covariance: \( S = P^- + R = 0.05 \).
  3. Gain: \( K = P^-/S = 0.04/0.05 = 0.8 \).
  4. Update: \( \hat z = 1.80 + 0.8(0.25) = 2.00 \) m.
  5. Covariance: \( P = (1-K)P^- = 0.008 \) ⇒ σ = 8.9 cm.
Read every number. K = 0.8: the sensor is twice as precise as the prior, so it gets 80% of the vote.

σ fell from 20 cm to 8.9 cm — sharper than either input. That is the whole point: two independent, imperfect views beat both.

Sanity check the innovation: 0.25 m against \( \sqrt S = 0.22 \) m ⇒ 1.1σ. Perfectly ordinary. Had it been 5σ, the honest response is to reject the measurement as an outlier, not to swallow it.
(2 min) — Repeat with a barometer instead: \( R = 0.25 \) (σ = 50 cm). What is \( K \) now, and what does the filter conclude? (\( K = 0.04/0.29 = 0.14 \) ⇒ \( \hat z = 1.835 \) m, σ = 18.6 cm. The filter politely ignores a sensor worse than its own prediction — automatically, with no special case in the code.)
Doing one step by hand with real numbers is the moment the Kalman filter stops being intimidating.

Detail, done properly

Outlier rejection: the guard every real filter has

Before accepting a measurement, test whether it is plausible under the filter's own belief:

\[ \text{accept if}\quad \frac{(z - H\hat{\mathbf x}^-)^2}{S} < \chi^2_{\text{thresh}} \;\;(\text{e.g. } 9 = 3\sigma) \]
  • A flow sensor crossing a shadow, a laser catching a table edge, a GPS multipath spike — all produce measurements the model says are impossible.
  • Rejecting them is not cheating: it is refusing to update on data inconsistent with everything else you know.
And the trap. Reject too eagerly and the filter becomes self-confirming: it ignores every correction that disagrees with its drift, and sails confidently into a wall.

The standard defence is a counter: if \( N \) consecutive measurements are rejected, the filter is probably wrong — inflate \( P \) and start accepting again. Humility, implemented.

Your Week-10 demonstration floor has a shiny patch and a shadow. Both will produce outliers. Whether your team's aircraft handles them gracefully or lurches is decided by this slide.

Tie directly to the demo floor; teams that implement gating see a visible difference.

Detail, done properly

Where the estimator sits in the loop — and what it costs

Sensors in, one belief out, every controller from Weeks 5–7 downstream of it. The dashed feedback is now closed through an inference, not a fact.

  • Order matters: estimate first, then control, within the same tick. Using last tick's estimate adds a full period of delay for free.
  • Rate matters: run the predict step at the full loop rate (200 Hz) and the update step whenever a measurement arrives (asynchronously). This is what 'multi-rate fusion' means in practice.
The budget on a real flight controller. Predict: a 12×12 matrix product, ~2 μs. Update: a small inverse, ~5 μs. Total well under 1% of a 200 Hz budget.

Estimation is cheap; the expensive part is getting it right. That asymmetry is why estimator bugs, not estimator cost, dominate real projects.
The microsecond budget reassures students worried about implementing this on an MCU.

Detail, done properly

What the controller should do when the estimate is poor

  • The filter publishes \( P \) — use it. A controller that ignores its own uncertainty acts confidently on nonsense.
  • Gain scheduling on covariance: when position σ passes a threshold, cut outer-loop gains and shrink the velocity limit. Fly slower when less sure.
  • Mode fallback: beyond a hard limit, abandon position hold and revert to altitude hold with level attitude. Drift gently rather than chase a fiction.
What commercial drones do. Lose GPS and a DJI aircraft announces 'switching to ATTI mode': it stops holding position, holds attitude, and hands the pilot the decision.

That is this slide, shipped. The estimator said 'I no longer know where I am', and the control system changed its ambitions instead of pretending.

For Week 10: if the GPS fixes arrive late — and on the team plant they will — the graceful behaviour is a slow drift, not a hunt. Deciding how much to trust a stale fix in advance, in code, is what separates a diagnosis from a surprise.

One level deeper

Why delay destabilises rather than merely annoys

A pure delay contributes phase lag proportional to frequency:

\[ \angle e^{-j\omega T_d} = -\omega T_d \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{at } \omega = 2.2,\; T_d = 0.06\,\text{s}: \; -7.6^\circ \]
  • Small at the outer loop's bandwidth — tolerable.
  • At the inner loop's 13.4 rad/s the same delay costs \( 46^\circ \) — most of a typical phase margin.
  • Hence: attitude from the fast IMU, never from a delayed vision pipeline.
The rule this gives you. Each loop must be fed by sensing at least an order of magnitude faster than itself. Week 6's timescale ladder was, all along, a sensor-latency budget in disguise — the cascade exists partly because our sensors run at different speeds.
Revealing the cascade as a latency budget retroactively explains Wk 6; it is the week's most unifying idea.

The instructor's aircraft

What the F570 actually runs: the explicit complementary filter

  1. Predict attitude by integrating the quaternion: \( \dot q = \tfrac12 q \otimes [0, \boldsymbol\omega] \).
  2. Predict which way 'down' should look, from the current quaternion: \( \hat{\mathbf v} = R^\top(0,0,-1)^\top \).
  3. Measure which way down does look: \( \hat{\mathbf a} = \mathbf a/\lVert\mathbf a\rVert \).
  4. Error as a rotation: \( \hat{\mathbf e} = \hat{\mathbf a} \times \hat{\mathbf v} \) — the axis and (sine of the) angle needed to reconcile them.
  5. Correct the gyro with a PI controller on that error: \( \boldsymbol\delta = k_p\hat{\mathbf e} + k_i\sum \hat{\mathbf e} \), then integrate \( \boldsymbol\omega + \boldsymbol\delta \).
Two things worth noticing. (a) The cross product is the error. Week 1's axis–angle representation, doing real work: \( \mathbf a \times \mathbf v \) is exactly the rotation that would align the two vectors — no Euler angles, no singularity.

(b) The integral term learns the gyro bias. \( k_i \sum \hat{\mathbf e} \) accumulates the persistent disagreement between gyro and gravity — which is the bias. This is the bias-state idea, implemented as an integrator rather than a Kalman state.

This is Mahony's filter, and it is what runs on small aircraft that cannot afford a full EKF. Three of our lecture ideas — quaternions, axis–angle error, and integral action — combined into twenty lines of C.

A beautiful synthesis slide: it shows three separate lecture threads converging in one real algorithm.

The engineering judgement. Attitude has a permanent absolute reference (gravity), so a fixed-gain blend suffices. Position has none indoors — flow gives velocity, which must be integrated — so you need a filter that tracks its own uncertainty and can fall back on prediction.

Different problems, different filters, on the same aircraft. The F570 uses ECF for attitude and a separate scheme for position, which is exactly the right split.

Before you leave

The traps, named

The trapThe truth
"Filter harder until the estimate looks clean."Smooth = delayed; delayed = phase loss; phase loss = the 1-Hz orbit. Controllers eat fresh, not pretty. Tune to the loop, not the plot.
"\(P\) is the error."\(P\) is the filter's claimed uncertainty. It is only meaningful if validated: the ±2σ band should bracket the true error ~95% of the time — a graded check in Lab 5.
"The accelerometer measures gravity."It measures specific force. In free-fall it reads zero; in a hard turn it lies about "down" exactly when you care. High-α or model the difference.
"KF assumptions fail on a quad, so it shouldn't work."The mechanism — trust weighted by uncertainty — is robust to the Gaussian fiction. Engineering happily runs on theorems slightly out of warranty, consciously.
Row 2's band-validation habit separates real estimation work from decorative filtering — it is in the lab rubric.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  • Sensors split into relative-smooth-drifting vs absolute-noisy-anchored; fusion marries them.
  • Complementary filter: one α, three lines, ships everywhere. Kalman: trust that moves with uncertainty.
  • \(K = P^-/(P^-+R)\) — the gain is a trust ratio. Dropout ⇒ coast and confess (P grows).
  • Over-smoothing is delay; delay is phase loss; phase loss is the orbit. Fresh beats pretty.
