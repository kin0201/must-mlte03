Optimal control got humans to the Moon before it got to your quad

First principles

From 'place the poles' to 'state what you want'

  • Week 5 gave you pole placement: pick \( \omega_n, \zeta \), solve for gains. It works — for one axis at a time.
  • Twelve states and four coupled inputs make 'pick the poles' an under-determined wish: which of the twelve should be fast? At what cost to the others?
  • LQR answers by changing the question. Instead of specifying where the poles go, you specify what you dislike — and the optimum places the poles for you.
The reframing, concretely. Pole placement asks: 'put the position pole at \( -2 \) and the attitude pole at \( -13 \)'.

LQR asks: 'I can tolerate 0.5 m of position error and 20° of tilt, and I have 2.4 N of spare thrust per motor.' The second is a statement about the mission; the first is a statement about mathematics. Engineers can defend the second one in a design review.
Framing LQR as a change of question (not a fancier algorithm) is what makes it click for students who already know pole placement.

Term by term

Reading \( Q \) and \( R \) as engineering statements

SymbolReads asUnits of the entrySet it from
\( Q_{ii} \)'how much I dislike error in state \( i \)'1/(state unit)²the largest deviation you would tolerate
\( R_{jj} \)'how expensive input \( j \) is'1/(input unit)²the actuator headroom you own
\( Q/R \) ratiothe only thing that matters—scale both, nothing changes
Bryson's rule, and why it works. \( Q_{ii} = 1/x_{i,max}^2 \), \( R_{jj} = 1/u_{j,max}^2 \).

Each term then contributes \( \approx 1 \) to the cost when that variable sits at its tolerance. It non-dimensionalises the problem, so metres, radians and newtons become comparable — which is the only honest way to add them.
On our aircraft. Position 0.5 m ⇒ \( Q = 4 \). Tilt 20° = 0.35 rad ⇒ \( Q = 8.2 \). Thrust headroom 9.6 N total ⇒ \( R = 0.011 \).

Now every number in the cost function traces to a physical tolerance you could defend to a customer.
The non-dimensionalisation argument is the intellectual content of Bryson's rule; without it the rule looks arbitrary.

Worked example · by hand

LQR on the vertical axis, start to finish

Two states \( (z, v_z) \), one input \( T \). Small enough to do completely.

  1. \( A = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix} \), \( B = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1/m\end{bmatrix} \) — a double integrator.
  2. Bryson: \( Q = \mathrm{diag}(1/0.5^2, 1/1^2) = \mathrm{diag}(4,1) \), \( R = 1/9.6^2 = 0.011 \).
  3. Solve the ARE numerically ⇒ \( K \approx [19.4,\ 10.9] \).
  4. Closed loop: \( s^2 + (K_2/m)s + K_1/m = s^2 + 16.8s + 29.8 \).
  5. \( \omega_n = 5.5 \) rad/s, \( \zeta = 1.54 \) — overdamped, ~1.2 s settle.
Now interrogate the answer. Overdamped and gentle. Is that what we wanted? For altitude near the ground — yes: overshoot means either a ceiling strike or a hard landing.

Want it faster? Tighten the tolerance: \( x_{max} = 0.2 \) m ⇒ \( Q_{11} = 25 \) ⇒ \( \omega_n \approx 8.7 \) rad/s. One number changed, one physical justification given, and the design moved.
(2 min) — What happens to \( K \) if you keep \( Q \) and multiply \( R \) by 100? Predict the direction before computing. (Control became 100× more expensive ⇒ gains shrink by roughly \( \sqrt{100} = 10 \) ⇒ \( \omega_n \) drops ~3×. The square-root scaling is worth remembering.)
A complete, hand-checkable LQR design. The 'interrogate the answer' step is what separates using a solver from doing engineering.

History, in one minute

Where 'modern control' came from

  • 1957–60 — Bellman's dynamic programming and Pontryagin's maximum principle arrive within three years of each other, from opposite sides of the Cold War.
  • 1960 — Kálmán publishes both the filter and the LQR duality. State space displaces frequency-domain methods for multivariable problems.
  • 1960s — Apollo. Fuel-optimal steering was not an academic exercise; the margins were measured in seconds of hover time.
  • Now — the same three-line recipe runs on a ¥419 aircraft. What changed is not the mathematics but the price of computing it.
Why the history is worth a minute. Because it explains the shape of the theory: LQR is quadratic and linear not because engineers like parabolas, but because that was the largest class solvable in closed form on 1960s hardware. Weeks 11–14 are what becomes possible when you can afford to solve harder problems every millisecond.
This slide reframes the whole second half of the course as 'what compute made possible', which is the honest historical story.

Block A · 00:10

Say what you want: the quadratic cost

\[ J = \int_0^\infty \Big( \underbrace{\mathbf{x}^\top Q\, \mathbf{x}}_{\text{state displeasure}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{u}^\top R\, \mathbf{u}}_{\text{effort displeasure}} \Big)\, dt \]
  • \(Q \succeq 0\): how much each state's deviation hurts. Diagonal in practice — one number per state.
  • \(R \succ 0\): how much control costs — battery, heat, saturation risk.
  • Only the ratio matters: scale both, same controller.
Why quadratic? Honest answer, master's edition: (1) penalizes big errors superlinearly, ignores tiny ones; (2) it's the largest class where the optimum stays a linear law — the math closes; (3) it linearizes the true nonlinear objective near hover, which is where we live.
Push on point 2: "optimal" always means optimal *for a stated J on a stated model*. Change J, change the controller. There is no free objectivity — LQR just makes the subjectivity explicit and low-dimensional.

Block A

The result: solve once, feed back forever

For \( \dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{u} \), the minimizing control is pure state feedback:

\[ \mathbf{u}^\ast = -K\mathbf{x}, \qquad K = R^{-1}B^\top P \]

where \(P \succ 0\) solves the algebraic Riccati equation:

\[ A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0 \]
What \(P\) is: the optimal cost-to-go, \( J^\ast(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{x}_0^\top P\,\mathbf{x}_0 \). A quadratic bowl over state space; the controller always slides downhill. (File this shape away — MPC's terminal cost, Wk 12, is exactly this bowl.)
In code, the whole theory is: 
P = scipy.linalg.solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)
K = np.linalg.solve(R, B.T @ P)
Conditions worth stating precisely once: (A,B) stabilizable, (A,Q^1/2) detectable → unique stabilizing solution. Our Wk-4 controllability check bought exactly this.

Formally, once

The guarantee, stated like adults — and \( Q,R \) with real units

Theorem (LQR existence/uniqueness). If \( (A,B) \) is stabilisable and \( (A, Q^{1/2}) \) detectable, the ARE has a unique stabilising \( P \succ 0 \); \( \mathbf u = -K\mathbf x \) with \( K = R^{-1}B^\top P \) minimises \( J \) and the closed loop is asymptotically stable. (Wk-4's controllability check was the entry ticket.)
Worked example (Bryson's rule on the quad's z-axis). Tolerances: height error 0.5 m, climb rate 1 m/s, per-motor headroom \( \Delta f = 2.4 \) N (4 × that in T): \[ Q_z = \mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{0.5^2}, \tfrac{1}{1^2}\Big) = \mathrm{diag}(4, 1), \qquad R_T = \tfrac{1}{9.6^2} \approx 0.011 \] Solve: \( K_z \approx [19.4,\ 10.9] \) ⇒ \( \omega_n \approx 5.5 \) rad/s, \( \zeta \approx 0.99 \) — a sane, nearly-critical altitude loop derived from stated tolerances, no folklore consumed. Change your tolerance, re-solve, done.
Remark (robustness — the famous fine print). Full-state LQR enjoys textbook margins (GM \( \in [\tfrac12, \infty) \), PM \( \ge 60^\circ \) per channel). The moment an estimator supplies \( \hat{\mathbf x} \) (LQG, next week) those guarantees vanish — Doyle 1978's abstract is one sentence: "There are none." Design margins must then be checked, not assumed.
The Bryson numbers are reproducible in the lab in 5 minutes — assign as the warm-up. Doyle's one-liner is the best-known abstract in control; quote it verbatim for flavour.

Block A · the 5-minute derivation sketch

Where Riccati comes from (dynamic programming)

1Bellman: the tail of an optimal path is optimal ⇒ define cost-to-go \( V(\mathbf x) \) The one idea. If the remainder weren't optimal, you'd swap it and do better — contradiction. Everything else is bookkeeping.
2Guess \( V = \mathbf x^\top P\, \mathbf x \) Why dare? Linear dynamics + quadratic cost keep quadratics closed under the recursion. Guess-and-verify is a *method*, not a cheat.
3HJB: \( 0 = \min_{\mathbf u}\big[\, \mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x + \mathbf u^\top R\mathbf u + \nabla V^{\top}(A\mathbf x + B\mathbf u) \,\big] \) Instantaneous consistency: present cost + drift of future cost must cancel along the optimum.
4\( \partial/\partial \mathbf u = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf u^\ast = -R^{-1}B^\top P \mathbf x \) The minimization is a parabola in u — differentiate, set to zero, done. Linear feedback wasn't assumed; it *emerged*.
5substitute back \( \Rightarrow A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0 \) The ARE, term for term. Riccati is just "the guess must be self-consistent", written out.
Why show you this at all: the same three moves — value function, one-step optimality, minimize-and-substitute — are Wk 11's MPC (finite horizon, constraints keep it numeric) and Wk 13's Bellman equation (unknown model makes it sampled). LQR is the one case with a closed form. Everything after is this slide, relaxed.
Do not grind the algebra live; the arc matters more than the steps. Full derivation: MIT 16.323 Lec 3–4, linked in teaching resources.

Detail, done properly

Solving the Riccati equation — what the solver is doing

  • The ARE is quadratic in the unknown matrix \( P \), so there is no formula — but there is structure.
  • Iterative view: run the finite-horizon recursion backwards from \( P_N = Q \) until it stops moving. That is literally dynamic programming, and it converges because the map is a contraction.
  • Eigen view: the stabilising \( P \) can be read off the stable eigenvectors of a \( 2n \times 2n \) Hamiltonian matrix — the route scipy.linalg.solve_continuous_are takes.
The iteration, in five lines. P = Q
for _ in range(2000):
  K = inv(R + B.T@P@B) @ B.T@P@A
  P = Q + K.T@R@K + (A-B@K).T@P@(A-B@K)

This is exactly what the LQR widget runs live, 800 iterations per slider move. The 'optimal' controller is a fixed point you can watch converge.

Backward-in-time recursion, converging to a fixed point, is the same computational skeleton as Week 13's value iteration. One algorithm, two costumes.

Showing the five-line iteration demystifies the solver and sets up the Wk-13 parallel explicitly.

Detail, done properly

\( P \) is the cost-to-go — and you can read it

What \( P \) means. \( J^\ast(\mathbf x_0) = \mathbf x_0^\top P \mathbf x_0 \): the total future cost of being at \( \mathbf x_0 \) and behaving optimally forever after. A quadratic bowl over state space.
Reading its entries. For the vertical axis, \( P \approx \begin{bmatrix} 21 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \).

• \( P_{11} = 21 \): being 1 m off costs 21 units.
• \( P_{22} = 4 \): moving at 1 m/s costs 4.
• \( P_{12} = 6 \): the cross term — being high and climbing is worse than either alone, because you must undo both.

Cross terms are where the interesting information lives.
  • The bowl's shape is the controller's worldview: it slides downhill on this surface, always.
  • Week 11 will reuse \( P \) as MPC's terminal cost — 'beyond my horizon, assume LQR takes over'.
  • Week 13 will learn this same object from experience, without ever writing \( A \) or \( B \).
Reading P's entries out loud — especially the cross term — turns an abstract matrix into a legible statement.

Detail, done properly

Integral action inside LQR: state augmentation

LQR is proportional in spirit: it drives \( \mathbf x \to \mathbf 0 \) but leaves a steady offset under a constant disturbance. The fix is not to bolt on an integrator — it is to put the integrator in the state:

\[ \mathbf x_{aug} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf x \\ \int e_z\,dt \end{bmatrix}, \quad A_{aug} = \begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf 0 \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_{aug} = \begin{bmatrix} B \\ \mathbf 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Now weight the integral state in \( Q \) and the optimisation designs the integral gain for you — correctly traded against everything else.
  • This is how production LQR/LQG controllers get offset-free tracking.
Why this is better than bolting one on. A bolted-on integrator is tuned separately and can fight the optimal gains. An augmented one is part of the optimisation, so the trade-off between steady accuracy and control effort is made once, coherently.

Cost: one more state, and \( Q \) grows by one entry. Cheap.
State augmentation is a genuinely useful trick students can apply immediately in the lab extension.

Block B · 01:00

LQR on the quadrotor: the recipe

  1. Model: Wk-4's \( (A,B) \) at hover — 12 states, 4 inputs, \( \Delta\mathbf{u} \) about trim.
  2. Structure: at hover, \(A\) block-decouples: x-pitch, y-roll, z, yaw. Design one \(K\) for all 12 states, or per-axis — same answer, and seeing why is a good exam question.
  3. Bryson's rule to start: \( Q_{ii}=1/x_{i,\max}^2 \), \( R_{jj}=1/u_{j,\max}^2 \) — "normalize by what you'd tolerate": 0.5 m position, 20° tilt, per-motor headroom to \(f_{\max}=4\) N.
  4. Iterate on the closed loop, not on gut: too slow → shrink the offending \(x_{\max}\); motors clipping → raise \(R\).
  5. Steady error? LQR is proportional in spirit — augment the state with \( \int e_z \) (and friends) to build the integrator into the design.
Full-state feedback \( \mathbf{u}=-K\mathbf{x} \) quietly assumes you have \( \mathbf{x} \). In quadsim you do. On the aircraft you have sensors. Week 8 fills exactly that gap — LQR + estimator = LQG.

One level deeper

Duality: the filter and the controller are the same problem

Control (LQR)Estimation (Kalman)

controllabilityobservability

Kálmán's observation, 1960. The two Riccati equations are the same equation with the matrices transposed. Design a controller, transpose the problem, and you have designed an estimator.

This is not a curiosity — it means every intuition you build this week transfers directly to next week. Expensive control ⇔ trusted model. Cheap control ⇔ trusted sensor. The Q/R dial you dragged in the widget is the same dial you will drag in the Kalman widget.

Two of the three great results of 1960 control theory, and they turn out to be one result seen from two sides.

Preview duality now so Wk 8 feels like a continuation rather than a new subject. Students find this genuinely elegant.

One level deeper

What 'optimal' does not mean

  • Not best for every trajectory — best on average, weighted by the cost you wrote.
  • Not robust to model error by construction — that came free, and only for full-state feedback.
  • Not respectful of limits — the optimisation is over an unconstrained input space.
  • Not unique as an engineering answer — a different \( Q, R \) is a different, equally 'optimal', controller.
The professional formulation. Never say 'we used an optimal controller'. Say: 'we minimised \( \int \mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x + \mathbf u^\top R\mathbf u \) with \( Q \) and \( R \) chosen from stated tolerances, on the hover linearisation.'

The second sentence is checkable, defensible, and reproducible. The first is marketing. Your final-project viva will reward the second and probe the first.

Every method in the second half of this course claims optimality with respect to something. Learning to ask 'optimal for what, on what model, subject to what?' is the transferable skill.

This slide is genuinely about intellectual honesty and directly prepares viva question C3.

Reading a \( K \) matrix like prose

For the lateral block \( (y, v_y, \phi, p) \) with input \( \tau_x \), a typical result:

\[ K \approx [\,-0.9,\; -1.4,\; 0.31,\; 0.043\,] \]
  • Signs first: position and velocity gains are negative because a positive \( y \) error needs a negative roll. A sign error here is Week 5's polarity problem, arriving through algebra instead of wiring.
  • Magnitudes: attitude gains are far larger relative to their states — attitude is the fast, high-authority axis, exactly as \( B \)'s \( 1/I \approx 435 \) predicted.
The ratio that reveals the architecture. \( K_\phi/K_y \approx 0.35 \) — but \( \phi \) is in radians and \( y \) in metres. Convert to comparable units and the attitude loop is roughly six times stiffer than the position loop.

LQR rediscovered the cascade's timescale separation on its own, from a cost function that never mentioned it. The architecture we imposed by hand in Week 6 is what optimality wanted anyway.
The most satisfying result of the week: the optimiser independently reproduces the hand-designed architecture.

Concept check

Design judgment

Your Lab-4 LQR beats PID on settle time but the motor traces show hard clipping at \(f_{\max}\) during the transient. Your report should conclude…
A. LQR wins — ship it
B. The comparison is invalid until \(R\) is raised (or \(x_{\max}\) relaxed) so the design respects the true actuator budget

Detail, done properly

Robustness margins: the good news, precisely stated

The classical LQR margins. For full-state feedback with \( R \) diagonal, every input channel enjoys: gain margin \( \in [\tfrac12, \infty) \) and phase margin \( \ge 60^\circ \). You may halve or arbitrarily increase any channel's gain and remain stable.
What that buys on hardware. Your motor thrust constant \( k_f \) is uncertain by perhaps ±15%; the battery sags 10% over a flight; a propeller wears. All of these are gain perturbations — and an infinite upper gain margin means none of them destabilises the loop.

That is a genuinely strong statement, and it is free: you did not design for robustness, you designed for a cost, and robustness arrived.

Compare with hand-tuned PID, where robustness is whatever your testing happened to cover. This is the concrete engineering value of an optimality principle.

And the fine print, restated. These margins hold for \( \mathbf u = -K\mathbf x \) with the true state. Feed an estimate instead (Week 8) and Doyle's 1978 result applies: there are no guaranteed margins for LQG. Robustness must then be checked, and recovered deliberately (loop-transfer recovery, or simply more conservative weights).
Give the concrete hardware reading of the margins; it converts a theorem into a reason they will trust the controller on their aircraft.

Detail, done properly

Discrete-time LQR — what actually runs on the aircraft

Your controller is a sampled system, so the honest design is discrete:

\[ \min \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(\mathbf x_k^\top Q_d \mathbf x_k + \mathbf u_k^\top R_d \mathbf u_k\big), \quad \mathbf x_{k+1} = A_d\mathbf x_k + B_d\mathbf u_k \]
  • \( A_d = e^{A\Delta t} \), \( B_d = \int_0^{\Delta t}e^{As}ds\,B \) — Week 4's discretisation, used in earnest.
  • scipy.linalg.solve_discrete_are; the gain is then applied as \( \mathbf u_k = -K_d\mathbf x_k \), held constant across the step.
Does it matter at 200 Hz?. Barely: \( \omega_n \Delta t \approx 0.07 \), so continuous and discrete gains differ by ~3%. At 50 Hz it matters (~12%), and at 20 Hz the continuous design can be visibly wrong.

Rule: if your loop rate is more than ~20× the closed-loop bandwidth, design continuous and sleep well. Otherwise, design discrete.
Gives them a decision rule rather than dogma; the 20× threshold is the practical takeaway.

Concept check

Two questions before the lab

Q1 — Two teams use identical \( Q \) but one scales \( R \) by \( 10^6 \) and also scales \( Q \) by \( 10^6 \). Do they get the same controller?

Yes — identical \( K \). Only the ratio enters the ARE. This is worth knowing because it means you can normalise your weights to whatever scale reads nicely without changing the design at all.

Q2 — Your LQR yields \( \zeta = 1.5 \) on altitude, which feels sluggish. A teammate proposes adding a derivative term on top of \( -K\mathbf x \). Respond.

There is already one — \( K \) contains the velocity gain. \( \mathbf u = -K\mathbf x \) with \( \mathbf x = (z, v_z) \) is PD, with gains chosen optimally. Wanting it faster means changing \( Q \) or \( R \), not bolting on a term the optimisation already accounted for.

Q2 is the conceptual leap: state feedback subsumes PD. Make sure they see K's second entry IS the D gain.

The aircraft in this room

Their design script — every line is this lecture

Ts = 0.005;                       % 200 Hz — same as quadsim
A  = [0 1; 0 0];   B = [0; 1/I_xx];     % roll subsystem (Wk 4)

    Q = [500 0; 0 10];  R = 5e4;  % preferences (Wk 7)
    K2 = dlqr(G,H,Q,R);           % discrete LQR  (Wk 7)

    y  = dlsim(G2,H,C,D,u,x0);    % simulate from 5 deg initial error
end
  • Line 5 is the one to notice: they test controllability before designing, and skip the axis if the rank is wrong. That is Week 4's abstract rank condition, used as a production guard.

Their initial condition. x0 = [0.0873; 0] — that is 5 degrees in radians. They simulate recovery from a 5° attitude error, which is precisely the step response you will produce in today's lab.

Weight-tuning mistakes, and what each looks like

MistakeWhat you seeWhyFix
All \( Q_{ii} = 1 \)erratic priorities1 metre and 1 radian treated as equally badBryson's rule, always
\( R \to 0 \)huge gains, motors clippingcontrol priced at zeroraise \( R \) until peak \( |u| \) fits

The authority principle. Weighting a state highly gives the controller more desire, not more power. If yaw authority is a tenth of roll's, a large \( Q_\psi \) just means yaw errors dominate a cost the actuators cannot satisfy — and the optimiser sacrifices axes it could have fixed.

Weight what you can control, in proportion to what you can control it with.
A diagnostic worth keeping. After every LQR design, print peak \( |u| \) for your worst expected disturbance. If it exceeds the actuator, the design is fiction no matter how pretty the eigenvalues look.
The 'desire vs power' distinction prevents a whole class of weight-tuning failures.

Before you leave

The traps, named

The trapThe truth
"Optimal means best, full stop."Optimal for the stated \(J\), on the stated model, unconstrained. Change any of the three and the crown moves. Every "optimal" in your report must name its \(J\).
"Bigger \(Q\) ⇒ faster, always."Until the commands hit \( f_{max} \) — then the theory is void and behaviour can degrade non-monotonically. Saturation is LQR's silent exit clause (and Wk-11's entrance).
"LQR replaces the cascade."It replaces the cascade's gain-picking. Architecturally you may still run it per-axis inside the same loops — same wires, chosen weights.
"State feedback is free — we have the state."quadsim has it; the aircraft has sensors. \( \mathbf u = -K\hat{\mathbf x} \) is a different animal (LQG) with different guarantees — next week is not optional.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  • LQR = state a preference \( (Q,R) \), solve the ARE, feed back \( \mathbf{u}=-K\mathbf{x} \). Unique, stabilizing, one matrix multiply at runtime.
  • \(P\) is the cost-to-go bowl — the same object MPC uses as its terminal cost.
  • Bryson's rule turns "what would I tolerate" into a first \(Q,R\). Iterate on the closed loop.
  • Blind spots, memorized: constraints · nonlinearity · needs full state → MPC (Wk 12), model mismatch (Wk 10), estimation (next week).
