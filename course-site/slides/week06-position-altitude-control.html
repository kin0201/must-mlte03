Recap → today

The inner loop holds attitude. So what?

  • Wk 5: \( \boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf{I}\,(K_p^{\text{att}}\mathbf{e}_\eta - K_d^{\text{att}}\boldsymbol\omega) \) — a tilted quad returns to level.
  • But level ≠ parked: it still drifts — nothing measures or corrects position.
  • And nothing holds height — thrust is whatever we hard-coded.
  • Today: wrap an outer loop around the inner one: position error → desired tilt + thrust.
Question to hold for 40 minutes:
A quad cannot accelerate sideways — its only force is thrust along its body z-axis. So how can it possibly move sideways?

First principles

Underactuation, stated precisely

Definition. A system is underactuated when it has fewer independent control inputs than configuration degrees of freedom. Quadrotor: 4 inputs, 6 DOF. You cannot command an arbitrary force and an arbitrary torque simultaneously.
What that forbids. There is no input that produces sideways force at constant attitude. To go right you must first roll right — and rolling right is itself a dynamic process taking ~0.1 s.

Position control is therefore never direct. It is always a request routed through attitude, which is why the architecture is a cascade rather than a preference.
  • Contrast: a fully-actuated hexrotor with tilted arms can translate at fixed attitude — 6 inputs, 6 DOF — and needs no cascade at all.
  • Contrast: a car is underactuated too (it cannot move sideways), which is why parallel parking is a manoeuvre rather than a translation.
The car-parking analogy lands instantly and is exactly right. It also explains why non-holonomic planning exists.

The cos-compensation term is a concrete, memorable piece of real autopilot code derived from Wk-1 geometry.

Detail, done properly

Velocity control: the loop hiding between position and attitude

Most autopilots do not go straight from position to acceleration. They insert a velocity loop:

\[ \mathbf p_d \;\xrightarrow{K_p^{pos}}\; \mathbf v_d \;\xrightarrow{PID}\; \mathbf a_{des} \;\xrightarrow{\text{tilt map}}\; \boldsymbol\eta_d \]
  • Why: velocity is where the limits live. Clamping \( \mathbf v_d \) gives a clean, interpretable speed limit — far easier to reason about than clamping an acceleration.
  • It also gives the pilot a natural manual mode: sticks command velocity, which is what 'position hold with stick input' means on a commercial drone.
The three-limit stack you will configure. \( v_{max} \) (speed), \( a_{max} \) (aggressiveness, via tilt), \( \eta_{max} \) (attitude, the hard safety cap). Each is a clamp at a different level, and together they define the aircraft's personality.

For the Week-10 demonstration: \( v_{max} = 1 \) m/s, \( \eta_{max} = 12^\circ \). Deliberately gentle, deliberately survivable.
The velocity loop is what students meet in real firmware; showing it now prevents confusion in Wk 9.

Worked example · by hand

Design the outer loop from the inner loop

Given the inner loop from Week 5 (\( \omega_i = 13.4 \) rad/s), design a position loop and predict the step response.

  1. Separation \( k = 6 \) ⇒ \( \omega_o = 13.4/6 = 2.2 \) rad/s.
  2. Choose \( \zeta = 0.8 \) (a little overshoot is acceptable outdoors; none indoors).
  3. \( K_p^{pos} = \omega_o^2 = 4.9 \), \( K_d^{pos} = 2\zeta\omega_o = 3.5 \).
  4. Step of 2 m ⇒ initial \( a_x = K_p(2) = 9.8 \) m/s² ⇒ \( \theta_d = \arctan(9.8/9.81) = 45^\circ \) — way past the clamp.
So the clamp does the work. With \( \eta_{max} = 12^\circ \), the first phase of the move is acceleration-limited, not gain-limited: the aircraft leans to 12°, accelerates at 2.1 m/s², and only when the error shrinks below \( 2.1/4.9 = 0.43 \) m does the linear design take over.

Large moves are slew-limited; small moves are PD-shaped. That two-regime behaviour is normal, intended, and visible in every real flight log.
(3 min) — How long does the 2 m step take? (Accelerate at 2.1 m/s² to \( v_{max} = 1 \) m/s in 0.48 s over 0.24 m; cruise 1.5 m in 1.5 s; decelerate symmetrically ⇒ ≈2.5 s total. You have just hand-computed a trajectory — which is exactly what Week 9 automates.)
This example unifies gains, clamps and trajectory planning in one calculation, and previews Wk 9 naturally.

Mental picture first

Why you cannot simply 'push' a quadrotor sideways

Level: thrust fights only gravity. Tilted: the same thrust splits into a vertical share that still holds you up and a horizontal share that accelerates you.

  • Try to command a sideways force directly and there is no actuator that produces one. The rotors only ever push along body-\( z \).
  • So every horizontal motion is a two-stage process: rotate first (fast, ~0.1 s), then let the rotated thrust act (slower).
  • That enforced sequencing is why the outer loop must be slower than the inner one — the delay is physical, not a tuning artefact.
(90 s) — At \( 12^\circ \) tilt, what fraction of thrust goes sideways, and what fraction of lift is lost? (\( \sin 12^\circ = 21\% \) sideways; \( 1-\cos 12^\circ = 2.2\% \) lift lost. Cheap in lift, useful in acceleration — which is why gentle tilts are such a good operating point.)
Picture and number before the algebra. The 21%/2.2% asymmetry explains why small tilts are so efficient.

Detail, done properly

What the outer loop is allowed to assume

  • Assumption 1: the inner loop reaches \( \boldsymbol\eta_d \) 'instantly'. True to within \( \omega_o/\omega_i \approx 17\% \) at our separation.
  • Assumption 2: thrust is delivered as commanded. True unless the mixer clamps.
  • Assumption 3: the state estimate is accurate and fresh. True in quadsim; an open question until Week 8.
  • Assumption 4: small angles, so \( \ddot x \approx g\theta \). True inside the tilt clamp.
Why listing assumptions is the work. Each one is a failure mode with a name. When the aircraft misbehaves, you do not guess — you ask which of the four broke.

Wobble at the outer loop's frequency? Assumption 1. Glitches during aggressive moves? Assumption 2. A slow orbit? Assumption 3 (Week 8's lesson, in advance). Odd behaviour only at large tilt? Assumption 4.

A controller is a set of assumptions with gains attached.
This slide is the debugging framework for the whole second half of the course. Say the closing sentence twice.

Block A · 00:10

The only force you have: \( T \) along body \(z\)

Newton in the world frame (Wk 2), thrust rotated by attitude:

\[ m\ddot{\mathbf{p}} \;=\; R(\boldsymbol\eta)\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\T\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\mg\end{bmatrix} \]
  • Level: thrust is vertical — no horizontal force at all.
  • Pitch nose-down by \( \theta \): a component \( T\sin\theta \) points forward.
  • Attitude is the steering wheel for position.
Expanding \(R\) for small angles (and yaw \( \psi=0 \)): \[ \ddot x \approx g\,\theta, \qquad \ddot y \approx -g\,\phi, \qquad \ddot z \approx \tfrac{T}{m}-g \] Each desired acceleration picks an attitude. That inversion is the outer loop.
Do the R e3 expansion on the board once — students should see where g·theta comes from, incl. the minus sign on phi (right-hand rule about x).

Derivation · click to advance interactive

Inverting the picture: acceleration → attitude

1\( m\ddot{\mathbf p} = R\,T\mathbf e_3 - mg\mathbf e_3 \) Week 2's result, unchanged. We now read it backwards: given a wanted p̈, what R and T?
2\( R\,\mathbf e_3 \approx \begin{bmatrix} \theta\cos\psi + \phi\sin\psi \\ \theta\sin\psi - \phi\cos\psi \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \) (small φ, θ) Linearize the direction of thrust. First-order in the tilt angles; yaw ψ stays exact — it just rotates which way "forward" is.
3\( \ddot x \approx g(\theta\cos\psi + \phi\sin\psi), \quad \ddot y \approx g(\theta\sin\psi - \phi\cos\psi) \) Substitute, use T≈mg. Two equations, two unknowns (φ, θ) — and they're linear.
4\( \theta_d = \tfrac{1}{g}(a_x\cos\psi + a_y\sin\psi), \;\; \phi_d = \tfrac{1}{g}(a_x\sin\psi - a_y\cos\psi) \) Solve — that's the outer loop's whole "tilt map". Clamp it, and the tilt limit becomes a horizontal-acceleration budget: a_max = g·tan(η_max).
5\( T = m(g + a_z) \) Vertical closes separately — with the mg feed-forward that fix #6 warned you never to make the integrator find.
Your turn (2 min) — with ψ = 90°, a request of a_x = 2 m/s² needs which attitude? Work it, then sanity-check the sign with your hands (nose is +x, ψ rotates it to +y…).
Step 4's a_max = g·tanη is the number safety officers quote at the demo. Make them memorize the 12° → ~2 m/s² pairing.

Block A

The outer loop, written down

PD on position gives a desired acceleration, then invert the small-angle map:

\[ \mathbf{a}_{\text{des}} = K_p^{\text{pos}}(\mathbf{p}_d-\mathbf{p}) + K_d^{\text{pos}}(\dot{\mathbf{p}}_d-\dot{\mathbf{p}}) \] \[ \theta_d = \frac{1}{g}\big(a_x \cos\psi + a_y \sin\psi\big), \qquad \phi_d = \frac{1}{g}\big(a_x \sin\psi - a_y \cos\psi\big), \qquad T = m\,(g + a_z) \]
Read it as a sentence: "to go that way, lean that way; to climb, push harder than gravity." The yaw terms just rotate the request into the body's heading.
Clamp before you send: \( |\phi_d|,|\theta_d| \le \eta_{\max} \) and \( 0 < T \le T_{\max} \). The tilt limit bounds horizontal acceleration — it is a safety knob, not a nuisance.
Note T = m(g + az): the m·g term is the hover feed-forward — the "output bias" from the seven fixes. Without it the integrator must find gravity by trial and error.

Block A

Why the cascade works: timescale separation

The bandwidth ladder. Each ×5–10 gap is an assumption the design leans on.

  • The outer loop assumes attitude tracking is instantaneous. That is only true if the inner loop is ~5–10× faster.
  • Inner \( \omega_n=\sqrt{K_p^{\text{att}}}\approx 13\,\text{rad/s} \) → outer should stay below ~2 rad/s ⇒ \( K_p^{\text{pos}} \lesssim 4 \).
  • Break the separation and the loops fight — an oscillation no gain can fix, because the architecture is wrong.
This is the single most common student failure in Lab 3: cranking outer Kp to 20 "to make it faster". The widget on the next-but-one slide lets them feel the boundary.

Formally, once

When is "the inner loop is instant" a theorem?

Claim (two-timescale separation, engineering form). If the inner loop tracks \( \boldsymbol\eta_d \) with bandwidth \( \omega_i \) and the outer loop is designed for bandwidth \( \omega_o \le \omega_i / k \), then the cascade behaves like the outer loop acting on a perfect attitude actuator, up to errors of order \( (\omega_o/\omega_i) \). Singular-perturbation theory makes this precise; practice settles for \( k \approx 5\text{–}10 \).
Worked example (our numbers). Inner \( \omega_i = 13.4 \) rad/s. With \( k=6 \): \( \omega_o \le 2.2 \) rad/s ⇒ outer \( K_p^{pos} = \omega_o^2 \le 4.9 \), \( K_d^{pos} = 2\zeta\omega_o \approx 3.1 \) (\( \zeta = 0.7 \)). The widget's "merge point" you found near \( K_p \approx 8 \) is this inequality failing — you measured a theorem.
Worked example (the safety budget, end to end). Tilt limit \( \eta_{max}=12^\circ \) ⇒ \( a_{max} = g\tan 12^\circ = 2.1 \) m/s². Across a 4 m arena, peak speed \( v = \sqrt{a\,d} \approx 2.9 \) m/s and full-stop distance \( v^2/2a \approx 2 \) m. One clamp constant fixes acceleration, top speed, and braking distance — that is why the demo config caps tilt, not speed.
"You measured a theorem" is the sentence to land — the widget experience becomes legitimate theory. The braking-distance number recurs verbatim in the Wk-10 safety briefing.

Block B · 01:00

Altitude: the channel that needs an integrator

  • Vertical: \( m\ddot z = T\cos\phi\cos\theta - mg \) — near hover, \( \ddot z \approx T/m - g \).
  • PD + hover feed-forward flies… until the battery sags, the payload changes, or props wear.
  • Any constant mismatch ⇒ constant height error. The integrator's one honest job: kill it.
\[ T = m\big(g + K_p e_z + K_d \dot e_z + K_i {\textstyle\int} e_z\,dt\big) \] with the integral clamped (fix #1) and frozen while on the ground — or takeoff winds it up before you lift.
Ask: why not put I on roll/pitch attitude too? (Answer: near-zero steady moment at hover; drift is handled by the position loop above it. On real airframes a small attitude-I trims CG offset — connect to F570 trim.)

Block B

Windup, in the one place it will bite you

Scenario: you command takeoff to 10 m. For the first seconds the error is huge; the integral charges; thrust saturates at \(T_{\max}\).

  • Arriving at 10 m, the integral is still full → sails through, overshoots meters.
  • Then the mirror image on the way back down.
The three-line cure (from the seven fixes):
  • clamp \( \int e_z \) to \( \pm E_{\max} \)
  • freeze it while \(|e_z|\) is large (integral separation)
  • freeze it while the output is saturated (conditional integration)
All three are one if each in student.py — and in the C firmware.
JiangXie slides 24–27 animate exactly this on a bench motor. On the aircraft, throttle windup is the classic "porpoising takeoff" — the Wk-9 troubleshooting table maps the symptom back here.

The asymmetry, in one number. To hold position, the required steady tilt is 0°. To hold altitude, the required steady thrust is 6.38 N — 40% of everything the aircraft has.

A controller that must discover that 6.38 N by integrating error will sag first and recover second. Feed it forward (fix 6) and the integrator is left with the small residue it is good at: battery sag, payload error, air density.
Why this shapes the lab. Your altitude channel is the one place in the whole course where you will genuinely need all three PID terms plus feed-forward plus anti-windup. It is the richest single loop you build — which is why Lab 3 grades it specifically.
Making altitude's specialness explicit prevents students from treating all three axes identically, the commonest Lab-3 design error.

Derivation · click to advance interactive

The altitude law, built term by term

1\( m\ddot z = T\cos\phi\cos\theta - mg \) Exact vertical dynamics from Week 2 — note the tilt factor, which is the term most treatments quietly drop.
2solve for the thrust that would produce a wanted \( \ddot z = a_z \): \( T = \dfrac{m(g + a_z)}{\cos\phi\cos\theta} \) Inversion, not approximation. The \( mg \) is feed-forward (fix 6); the denominator is the tilt compensation that stops the aircraft sagging when it leans.
3choose \( a_z = K_p e_z + K_d \dot e_z + K_i \!\int\! e_z\,dt \) Now, and only now, a PID. Everything the model knows has already been supplied; feedback handles what it does not know.
4clamp the integral; freeze it while disarmed or saturated Fixes 1 and 2. Without this, a take-off command charges the integral on the ground and the aircraft leaps. This is the single most common first-flight incident.
5finally clamp \( T \in [T_{min}, 4f_{max}] \) and report saturation upward Physics has the last word — and the controller must be told, so its integrator can stop. The loop is now honest about its own limits.

Five steps, and four of them are engineering rather than mathematics. That ratio is typical, and it is why the seven fixes are not an appendix to the subject.

Step 2's inversion structure (feed-forward + compensation, then feedback) is the template for every well-built loop.

Detail, done properly

Yaw: low authority, high consequence

  • Yaw does not move the aircraft — but it rotates the frame in which the outer loop thinks. Get \( \psi \) wrong and 'forward' points somewhere else.
  • Authority is \( c/l \approx 1/10 \) of roll (Week 3), and \( I_{zz} \approx 1.7 I_{xx} \) (Week 2). Roughly 17× slower than roll, from two independent causes.
  • So: gentle gains, generous tolerance, and never demand fast heading changes during a position manoeuvre.
The wrap bug, precisely. \( \psi = 179^\circ \), command \( -179^\circ \). Naive: \( e = -358^\circ \) ⇒ a full-authority pirouette the long way round.
Wrapped: \( e = +2^\circ \) ⇒ a nudge.

e = atan2(sin(r-y), cos(r-y)) — one line, and it must appear in every angular error you ever compute, including the attitude loop's.
(2 min) — Your outer loop computes \( a_x = 2 \) m/s² in the world frame while the aircraft faces \( \psi = 90^\circ \). Which body-axis tilt does that require? (Roll, not pitch: the tilt map's \( \psi \) terms rotate the request into the body frame. Skip that rotation and your quad flies at right angles to the command — a spectacular and instructive bug.)
The right-angle failure mode is memorable and is exactly what happens when students drop the psi terms from the tilt map.

Detail, done properly

Code walkthrough: the full cascade in thirty lines

def control(self, t, x, ref):
    # ---------- OUTER: position -> desired acceleration ----------
    e_p   = ref.pos - x.pos
    v_d   = np.clip(self.kp_pos * e_p, -self.v_max, self.v_max)   # velocity limit
    a_des = self.kp_vel*(v_d - x.vel) + ref.acc                    # + feedforward (Wk 9)

    # ---------- ALTITUDE: its own PID + gravity feedforward -------
    e_z    = ref.pos[2] - x.pos[2]
    self.iz = clamp_if_not_saturated(self.iz + e_z*self.dt, self.iz_max)
    a_z    = self.kp_z*e_z + self.kd_z*(0 - x.vel[2]) + self.ki_z*self.iz
    T      = self.m*(self.g + a_z) / max(0.5, cos(x.phi)*cos(x.theta))

    # ---------- TILT MAP: acceleration -> attitude setpoint -------
    c, s   = cos(x.psi), sin(x.psi)
    th_d   =  (a_des[0]*c + a_des[1]*s) / self.g
    ph_d   =  (a_des[0]*s - a_des[1]*c) / self.g
    th_d, ph_d = clamp(th_d, self.tilt_max), clamp(ph_d, self.tilt_max)

    # ---------- INNER: your Week-5 attitude loop, unchanged -------
    tau = self.attitude.step([ph_d, th_d, ref.yaw], x.euler, x.omega)
    return np.array([T, *tau])
  • Four blocks, in the order the signal flows. If you can name what each block would do wrong when broken, you can debug this loop.
  • Note max(0.5, ...) in the thrust divide — a guard against the compensation exploding past 60° tilt. Small, defensive, necessary.
The line to notice. self.attitude.step(...) — Week 5's controller is called unchanged. That is what a cascade buys: the inner loop never learns that an outer loop exists.
Walk block by block, asking 'what breaks if this line is wrong?'. The unchanged inner loop is the architectural payoff.

Detail, done properly

Tuning order: why outside-in never works

  1. Inner loop first, alone. Freeze the outer loop's output at zero tilt. Tune \( K_p^{att}, K_d^{att} \) against step disturbances until \( \zeta \approx 1 \).
  2. Altitude second, still alone. Hold position off; tune \( K_p^z, K_d^z \), then add \( K_i^z \) last and smallest.
  3. Outer position third, starting at a quarter of the separation-rule value and rising until the first sign of interaction.
  4. Yaw last, gently — it disturbs nothing else and is disturbed by everything.
Why the order is forced. Each loop's plant includes the loops inside it. Tune the outer loop against an untuned inner loop and you are fitting gains to a plant that will not exist tomorrow — every inner change invalidates the outer tuning.

Inside-out is not a preference; it is the only order in which each measurement means something.
The same order on a real aircraft. A flight-test programme enforces it physically: polarity → tethered attitude → altitude hold → free hover with position. The gates are the tuning order, made into a safety procedure — and the reason the instructor's demonstration in Week 5 stayed on the stand.
Revealing that the safety gates and the tuning order are the same sequence is satisfying and makes both feel less arbitrary.

Two questions before the lab

Q1 — You double \( K_p^{pos} \) and the aircraft becomes less accurate at holding position. Explain.

The outer loop is now fast enough to excite the inner one. Timescale separation has eroded; the two loops fight, producing an oscillation whose amplitude is worse than the drift you were trying to remove. More gain is not more control — it is a different architecture, badly.

Q2 — Your quad holds altitude perfectly in still air but sinks 20 cm every time it accelerates horizontally. One missing line. Which?

The \( 1/(\cos\phi\cos\theta) \) thrust compensation. Tilting spends lift on horizontal acceleration; without compensation the altitude loop only notices after the aircraft has already dropped, and then chases. Feed-forward what geometry already tells you.

One level deeper

Feedforward, previewed: the outer loop's next upgrade

Today's outer loop is purely reactive: it acts only after a position error exists. For a moving target that guarantees a lag.

\[ \mathbf a_{des} = \underbrace{\ddot{\mathbf p}_r}_{\text{feedforward (Wk 9)}} + \underbrace{K_p(\mathbf p_r - \mathbf p) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf p}_r - \dot{\mathbf p})}_{\text{today's feedback}} \]
  • The reference's own acceleration is known — so hand it to the controller instead of making feedback rediscover it.
  • Today \( \ddot{\mathbf p}_r = 0 \) because our references are constants. Week 9 makes them move.
The size of the effect. Tracking a circle of radius 1.2 m at 0.8 rad/s needs \( a = 0.77 \) m/s² of centripetal acceleration. Feedback alone must generate that from error: \( e = a/K_p = 0.77/4.9 = 0.16 \) m of permanent lag.

With feedforward, that error goes to essentially zero. Sixteen centimetres, bought by one term you already know how to compute.
Previewing feedforward here, with a number, makes Wk 9 feel like a promised payoff rather than new material.

Detail, done properly

Landing and take-off: the two manoeuvres that break controllers

  1. Take-off. On the ground, position error is large and thrust is below hover — the integrator charges while nothing moves. Cure: freeze I until thrust exceeds ~80% of hover, then release.
  2. Ground effect. Below ~0.3 m the aircraft gains 5–15% thrust unexpectedly. A well-tuned altitude loop handles it; a slow one balloons.
  3. Touchdown. Once a leg touches, the model is wrong — the aircraft is no longer a free body. Cure: detect landing (low thrust + low descent rate + stable altitude) and disarm, rather than letting the loop fight the floor.
Why landing detection matters. Without it, the altitude loop sees 'still above target' after touchdown, keeps commanding thrust, and the aircraft tips over on its legs — the classic 'landed then flipped' incident.

Every real autopilot has a landing detector, and it is nearly always three conditions AND-ed together with a hold-time. Simple, and it prevents a whole category of embarrassment.

Your Week-10 L4 task requires automatic take-off and landing. These three paragraphs are most of the work.

Directly actionable for the demo; landing detection is the piece teams most often forget.

Position hold in the wild: what 'GPS hold' really does

  • A commercial drone's 'position hold' is exactly today's cascade, with GPS+baro+IMU fusion feeding it (Week 8) and a velocity loop in the middle.
  • The pilot's sticks do not command angles — they command velocity setpoints, which the outer loop tracks. Release the sticks and the setpoint is zero: the aircraft brakes and holds.
  • Indoors, GPS is replaced by optical flow — the same loop, a different sensor, an order of magnitude less accuracy.
Why your aircraft is harder than a DJI. A Mavic holds position to ~10 cm outdoors with GPS+RTK and a downward camera array. Your aircraft holds ~30 cm indoors with a single flow sensor.

The controller is not the difference — the sensing is. That is worth remembering when comparing your Week-10 demonstration with a consumer product: you are running the same architecture on a tenth of the information.
Manages expectations honestly and reinforces that Wk 8 (sensing) is where the real difficulty lives.

Detail, done properly

One loop, four failure modes — a diagnostic decision tree

  1. Does attitude track its setpoint cleanly? No ⇒ the problem is inner-loop; go back to Week 5. Yes ⇒ continue.
  2. Is the position error oscillating at ~0.5 Hz? Yes ⇒ outer gains too hot (timescale separation).
  3. Is altitude coupled to horizontal manoeuvres? Yes ⇒ missing tilt compensation.
  4. Does it drift steadily in one direction? Yes ⇒ bias: CoM offset, trim, or wind. Add clamped I.
Why a decision tree, not a checklist. Because the first question partitions the problem. Most wasted debugging time is spent tuning an outer loop whose inner loop was never right — and the tree makes that impossible to skip.

Adopt this habit generally: before tuning any loop, verify that everything inside it already works. It is the same principle as the hardware gates.
Explicitly connecting the debugging tree to the hardware gate sequence reinforces both.

The instructor's aircraft

Feed-forward, taken seriously: the F570's base throttle

// weight (grams) -> throttle, PID firmware
static uint16_t weight_to_throttle(float weight, float height)
{
    weight = weight + height * 14.0f;      // (!) tether cable: 14 g per metre
    return 156 + 6.8f*weight
               - 0.0232f*weight*weight
               + 0.0000512f*weight*weight*weight;
}
  • A cubic fit of the inverse thrust curve — the exact \( mg \) feed-forward this lecture argued for, but calibrated rather than assumed.
  • And look at line 3. The tethered aircraft drags its own power cable; every metre of altitude adds 14 g of hanging cable to be lifted.
Why that line is the best teaching example in the whole manual. The mass being lifted is a function of the state — it grows with height. A pure feedback loop would discover this as a persistent altitude error and fight it with the integrator, forever.

Instead the vendor modelled it: one term, \( 14h \), and the disturbance vanishes before the controller ever sees it.

Feed-forward what you know; feed back what you don't. Fix 6, in a shipping product, doing real work.
This is the single most instructive line in the vendor's code. Make sure everyone sees that the cable weight is state-dependent and therefore perfect feed-forward material.

The traps, named

"Make the position loop faster — raise its gains."The outer loop's speed is rented from the inner loop's. Past \( \omega_i/5 \) you are not tuning any more, you are dismantling the architecture.

What to remember

  • Tilt to translate: \( \theta_d \approx a_x/g \), \( \phi_d \approx -a_y/g \), \( T=m(g+a_z) \) — attitude is position's actuator.
  • The cascade lives on timescale separation (~5–10×). Break it and no gain helps.
  • Integral action belongs on altitude, clamped, frozen on the ground.
