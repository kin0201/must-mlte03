This machine should not be able to fly

The software stack

What runs where, and how fast

The rate ladder — the same picture whether the vehicle is 200 g or 2 tonnes.

  • 1 kHz — sensor sampling, IMU filtering.
  • 200–500 Hz — rate loop (the innermost control law).
  • 50–200 Hz — attitude loop. Week 5.
  • 10–50 Hz — position / altitude loop. Week 6.
  • 1–10 Hz — guidance, mission logic. Week 9.
Why the ladder matters. Each layer assumes the layer beneath it is fast enough to be invisible. That single assumption is what lets us design four small controllers instead of one impossible twelve-state one — and violating it is the cause of an entire family of oscillations you will meet in Week 6.
Plant the ladder now; it reappears in Wk 6 (design), Wk 8 (sensor rates), Wk 12 (MPC scheduling). Three appearances of one idea is how it sticks.

Before anything flies

Rules, weight classes, and the one number that matters

  • Mass is the regulatory axis. Most jurisdictions — mainland China, Macau, EU, US — draw their first line at 250 g. Below it, requirements are light; above it, registration and pilot competency kick in.
  • The instructor's demonstrator is well under 250 g by design. That is not an accident; it is what makes a classroom demonstration legally and practically simple.
  • In Macau, outdoor operations fall under the Civil Aviation Authority (AACM). Nothing in this course flies outdoors, and only the instructor flies at all — the two demonstrations (Weeks 5 and 10) are inside this room.
The rule that never bends. Indoors, outdoors, 200 g or 20 kg: a spinning propeller is a blade. That is one reason this course keeps students in simulation and lets only the instructor fly — twice, behind a line, with a kill switch. Safety here is a procedure, not an attitude, and the simulator inherits the same discipline: an abort edge in every mission, tested before it is needed.

Prerequisite, made explicit

Vectors, bases, and coordinates are three different things

One physical arrow, two bases, two sets of numbers. R is the dictionary between the two descriptions — not a change to the arrow.

  • A vector is a geometric object — a displacement, a velocity, a force. It exists before anyone chooses axes.
  • A basis is a choice of three reference directions.
  • Coordinates are the three numbers you get after choosing a basis. Change the basis and the numbers change — the vector does not.
Example. The thrust of your quad is one physical arrow. In body coordinates it reads \( (0,0,T)^\top \); in world coordinates it reads \( R\,(0,0,T)^\top \). Same arrow, two descriptions. Ninety per cent of frame bugs are a program treating the numbers as if they were the arrow.

Notation we will hold to: a subscript names the frame the coordinates are in — \( \mathbf v_W \) versus \( \mathbf v_B \). When a symbol has no subscript, the frame must be obvious from context, or the equation is not finished.

Worth the four minutes. Every later confusion — Euler rates vs body rates, world vs body accelerations — is this distinction failing.

Prerequisite, made explicit

A matrix is a machine that maps basis vectors

For any matrix \( M \), the product \( M\mathbf e_1 \) is exactly the first column of \( M \); \( M \mathbf e_2 \) the second; \( M \mathbf e_3 \) the third.

\[ M = \big[\; M\mathbf e_1 \;\big|\; M\mathbf e_2 \;\big|\; M\mathbf e_3 \;\big] \]

So to read a matrix, ask: where does it send each axis?

Read a rotation this way. If \( R \) takes body coordinates to world coordinates, then column 1 of \( R \) is the body's x-axis (the nose) written in world coordinates. Column 3 is the body's z-axis — the direction thrust points. That is why \( R\,(0,0,T)^\top \) is just \( T \times \) (third column).
(90 s) — Take \( R = R_z(90^\circ) \). Without multiplying anything, write down where the nose points in world coordinates. (Column 1 = \( (0,1,0)^\top \): the nose points along world-y.)
This single habit — read columns, do not multiply — makes rotation matrices legible for the rest of the course.

Prerequisite, made explicit

Orthogonal matrices: what rotations preserve

Definition. \( M \) is orthogonal if \( M^\top M = I \); equivalently its columns are mutually perpendicular unit vectors.
  • Lengths are preserved: \( \lVert M\mathbf v \rVert = \lVert \mathbf v \rVert \). A rotated velocity has the same speed.
  • Angles are preserved: \( (M\mathbf a)\cdot(M\mathbf b) = \mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b \).
  • Inversion is free: \( M^{-1} = M^\top \) — no determinant, no solve, one transpose.
Why this matters computationally. Going world→body is \( R^\top \), which costs nothing. Your estimator will do this thousands of times a second. It is also a numerical hygiene tool: if you ever compute an \( R \) and \( R^\top R \) drifts from \( I \), your integration has gone wrong — a cheap assertion worth putting in the code.
The R^T R = I assertion is a genuinely useful debugging tool; suggest they add it to their lab code today.

The two frames

World vs body — and z is UP

  • World frame \(\{W\}\): fixed to the ground, ENU / z-up. Gravity points \(-z\).
  • Body frame \(\{B\}\): glued to the airframe. Thrust is always \(+\)body-\(z\).
  • The rotation matrix \(R\) carries a body vector into the world: \( \mathbf{v}_W = R\,\mathbf{v}_B \).
  • This is exactly how the sim resolves thrust & gravity into world acceleration.
Translational dynamics (from quadsim/dynamics.py): \[ \mathbf a = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-g\end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{m}\,R\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\T\end{bmatrix} \] Gravity is \(-z\); thrust \(T\) lives on \(+\)body-\(z\) and \(R\) tilts it into the world.
Convention call-out: Beard & McLain / the UAVbook use NED (z-down, gravity +z). We are z-up. If you lift an equation from a NED source, flip the z sign — never mix the two in one derivation.

The rotation matrix

\(R\) is a member of \(SO(3)\)

A rotation matrix is not any \(3\times3\) array. Its columns are the body axes \(\mathbf{x}_B,\mathbf{y}_B,\mathbf{z}_B\) expressed in world coordinates, and it obeys two laws:

\[ R^{\top}R = I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R^{-1}=R^{\top}, \qquad \det R = +1 \]
Orthonormal — the body axes stay unit-length and mutually perpendicular. The inverse rotation is just the transpose: no matrix solve needed.
\(\det=+1\) — a proper rotation, no reflection. \(\det=-1\) would flip handedness (a mirror), which no real rigid body does.

In today's lab you'll feed angles to rotation_matrix(...) and check both laws numerically.

Formally, once

Nine numbers, six constraints, three freedoms

Definition (the rotation group). \( SO(3) = \{ R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3} : R^\top R = I,\; \det R = +1 \} \). Orthonormality is 6 scalar constraints on 9 entries — leaving 3 degrees of freedom, which is why any minimal attitude parametrisation has exactly 3 numbers.
  • Columns of \(R\) = the body axes written in world coordinates. Read a rotation matrix by its columns — always.
  • \( R^{-1} = R^\top \): inverting a rotation is free. (Your Wk-8 estimator leans on this daily.)
  • \( \det R = +1 \) excludes reflections — a quad cannot turn into its mirror image.
Worked example. Pure yaw \( \psi = 90^\circ \): \( R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \). Column 1 = body-x expressed in world = \( (0,1,0)^\top \): the nose now points along world-y. Check: \( R\,(1,0,0)^\top = (0,1,0)^\top \). Do this column-reading once by hand and \(R\) stops being a wall of numbers.
The 9−6=3 count is a 60-second argument that pays off twice: it explains why Euler angles exist at all, and why any 3-number chart must have a singularity somewhere (Wk-1's gimbal lock) while the 9-number R never does.

A distinction that bites

Rotating a vector vs re-describing it

The object turns, or the frame turns. Same matrix, opposite stories — subscripts are what keep them apart.

Active (rotation). The object physically turns; the frame stays. \( \mathbf v' = R\,\mathbf v \), both in the same frame. Example: the quad banks.
Passive (coordinate change). The object is fixed; you describe it in a different frame. \( \mathbf v_W = R\, \mathbf v_B \). Example: expressing thrust in world coordinates.

The same matrix serves both readings — which is exactly why the confusion survives. The cure is to never write \( R \) alone: write \( R_{W\leftarrow B} \) when frames are in play, and let the subscripts cancel like units:

\[ \mathbf v_W = R_{W\leftarrow B}\, \mathbf v_B, \qquad R_{W\leftarrow B} = R_{B\leftarrow W}^{\top} \]
Course convention, fixed now: \( R \) means body→world unless a subscript says otherwise. quadsim.dynamics.rotation_matrix() returns this one.
The subscript-cancellation trick is what professional robotics code does (T_world_body). Recommend they name variables that way from day one.

Building blocks

The three elementary rotations, derived once

Rotate about \( z \) by \( \psi \). The z-axis is untouched; x and y turn in their plane:

\[ R_z(\psi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0 \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad R_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad R_x(\phi) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\ 0 & \sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Check column 1 of \( R_z \): \( (\cos\psi, \sin\psi, 0)^\top \) — the x-axis swung into the xy-plane by \( \psi \). Correct.
  • Notice \( R_y \) has its minus sign in the lower left, not the upper right, unlike the other two.
The sign trap. That \( R_y \) asymmetry is real and is the single most common hand-derivation error. It follows from the cyclic order \( x \to y \to z \to x \): the y-rotation maps \( z \to x \), reversing the pattern. When in doubt, rebuild the column by asking where each axis goes.
Derive R_z live on the board with a drawing; state the R_y sign trap explicitly — it will save marks in the exam and hours in the lab.

Worked example · by hand

Composition, and the proof that order matters

Two 90° rotations, two orders, two completely different attitudes — one sideways, one at the floor.

Take \( \phi = 90^\circ \) (roll), \( \theta = 90^\circ \) (pitch), yaw zero. Compare the two orders:

Pitch-then-roll: \( R_x R_y \). \( R_y(90^\circ) \) sends \( \mathbf e_1 \mapsto (0,0,-1)^\top \) (nose down). Then \( R_x(90^\circ) \) sends \( (0,0,-1) \mapsto (0,1,0) \).
Net: nose \( \to \) world \( +y \).
Roll-then-pitch: \( R_y R_x \). \( R_x(90^\circ) \) leaves \( \mathbf e_1 \) alone (roll does not move the nose). Then \( R_y(90^\circ) \) sends \( \mathbf e_1 \mapsto (0,0,-1)^\top \).
Net: nose \( \to \) world \( -z \), straight down.

Two identical angle pairs, two completely different attitudes — one pointing sideways, one pointing at the floor. \( R_x R_y \ne R_y R_x \), and no amount of care with the numbers rescues an unstated convention.

(2 min) — Do the same pair with \( 10^\circ \) instead of \( 90^\circ \). How different are the results? (Tiny — order matters at second order in the angles, which is precisely why small-angle work is forgiving and aggressive flight is not.)
The 10-degree follow-up is the important half: it explains why sloppy conventions survive gentle flight and explode in aggressive flight.

The full matrix

\( R(\phi,\theta,\psi) \), all nine entries

\[ R = R_z(\psi)R_y(\theta)R_x(\phi) = \begin{bmatrix} c_\psi c_\theta & c_\psi s_\theta s_\phi - s_\psi c_\phi & c_\psi s_\theta c_\phi + s_\psi s_\phi \\ s_\psi c_\theta & s_\psi s_\theta s_\phi + c_\psi c_\phi & s_\psi s_\theta c_\phi - c_\psi s_\phi \\ -s_\theta & c_\theta s_\phi & c_\theta c_\phi \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Do not memorise this. Memorise how to rebuild it: three elementary matrices, multiplied right to left.
  • The third column is the one that earns its keep all term — it is where thrust points.
The column that matters. \( R\mathbf e_3 = (c_\psi s_\theta c_\phi + s_\psi s_\phi,\; s_\psi s_\theta c_\phi - c_\psi s_\phi,\; c_\theta c_\phi)^\top \).
Week 6 linearises exactly this vector to get the tilt map. Every 'lean to move' argument in the course is one Taylor expansion of these three entries.
Flag now that they will meet the third column again in Wk 6; students appreciate knowing which piece of an ugly matrix will actually be used.

The approximation the whole course leans on

Small angles: \( R \approx I + \widehat{\boldsymbol\eta} \)

The cost of sin θ ≈ θ, measured. Our tilt band sits inside the 1–3% region; racing does not.

Set \( \cos \approx 1 \), \( \sin \approx \) angle, drop products of two small angles:

\[ R \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\psi & \theta \\ \psi & 1 & -\phi \\ -\theta & \phi & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\psi & \theta \\ \psi & 0 & -\phi \\ -\theta & \phi & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • The correction term is skew-symmetric — that structure is not a coincidence; it is the tangent space of \( SO(3) \) at the identity.
  • In this regime rotations do commute (to first order), which is why gentle flight forgives convention mistakes.
  • Third column \( \approx (\theta, -\phi, 1)^\top \) ⇒ \( \ddot x \approx g\theta \), \( \ddot y \approx -g\phi \).
Where the price is paid. Accurate to about 1% at \( 15^\circ \), 5% at \( 30^\circ \), 10% at \( 45^\circ \). Our tilt limit (12–25°) sits comfortably inside; a racing turn does not — which is the honest reason the aggressive-tracking example (M2, Week 11) cannot be flown by a hover-linearised design.
This slide is the bridge from Wk 1 to Wk 4 and Wk 6. Say explicitly: 'you have just derived the tilt map three weeks early'.

Mental picture first

Three turns, in order, each about a new axis

Three elementary rotations, applied to ever-newer axes — yaw sets heading, pitch lifts the nose, roll banks.

Say it as a sentence before the formulas: face your heading (ψ), lift your nose (θ), bank your wings (φ). The order is a convention — and conventions only work if nobody improvises.

Have the room do it with a flat hand: yaw, pitch, roll. Then swap the order and watch hands disagree — non-commutativity by kinesthetics.

Building R

Intrinsic ZYX: yaw → pitch → roll

The course builds \(R\) (body→world) by composing three elementary rotations in the order yaw \(\psi\), then pitch \(\theta\), then roll \(\phi\) — this is the 3-2-1 sequence:

\[ R(\phi,\theta,\psi) = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi) \]
This is exactly what quadsim.dynamics.rotation_matrix(phi, theta, psi) returns. Order matters: rotations do not commute, so \(R_z R_y R_x \neq R_x R_y R_z\). State always the sequence.
Show on the board that swapping the order gives a different orientation. The state vector stores (phi, theta, psi) at x[6:9]; the function signature takes them in roll-pitch-yaw argument order but composes Z·Y·X. Make sure students read the dynamics.py docstring.

The inverse problem

Getting \( (\phi,\theta,\psi) \) back out of \( R \)

Firmware stores \( R \) or a quaternion; humans want angles. Read them off the entries that isolate one angle each:

\[ \theta = -\arcsin(R_{31}), \qquad \phi = \operatorname{atan2}(R_{32}, R_{33}), \qquad \psi = \operatorname{atan2}(R_{21}, R_{11}) \]
  • Use atan2, never atan(y/x): atan2 keeps the quadrant, so \( 170^\circ \) does not silently become \( -10^\circ \).
  • Row 3 gives pitch alone because \( R_{31} = -\sin\theta \) — the one entry with no \( \psi \) in it.
At the singularity. When \( \cos\theta \to 0 \), both atan2 arguments for \( \phi \) and \( \psi \) go to zero: \( 0/0 \). Only the sum or difference \( \phi \pm \psi \) is recoverable — the exact statement of gimbal lock, in code rather than in pictures.
atan2 discipline is a genuine bug-prevention lesson; ask who has been bitten by a bare atan before.

Know the zoo

There are twelve Euler conventions and everyone picks a different one

ConventionSequenceUsed by
ZYX (3-2-1), intrinsicyaw → pitch → rollaerospace, this course, most autopilots
ZXZ (3-1-3)precession → nutation → spinclassical mechanics, orbital dynamics
XYZ extrinsicabout fixed world axessome CAD and graphics tools
Tait–Bryan vs proper Eulerthree distinct axes vs first = lastthe naming split behind most confusion
  • Intrinsic = each rotation about the new (already rotated) axis. Extrinsic = all about the original fixed axes.
  • Fact worth knowing: intrinsic ZYX and extrinsic XYZ give the same matrix with the order reversed. That equivalence is why two correct-looking sources appear to contradict each other.
Practical rule. When you import an equation, a dataset, or a library function, the first question is not 'is it right?' but 'which convention?'. SciPy's Rotation.from_euler('ZYX', ...) in capitals means intrinsic; lowercase means extrinsic. One character.
The SciPy capitalisation detail is a real, checkable gotcha that lands well — several students will have hit it already.

Mental picture first

Where "gimbal lock" got its name

  • Apollo 13's IMU used a physical three-gimbal platform — the crew actively steered around gimbal lock during the emergency return.
  • Our "gimbals" are the three Euler rotations. At pitch \( \pm 90^\circ \) the roll and yaw rings align: two knobs, one motion.
  • Watch the animation until you can say which ring becomes redundant — then the algebra on the next slide is a formality.
Mental model before math — let the gif loop twice in silence. Apollo detail is true and lands well with engineers.

The catch

Gimbal lock at pitch \(=\pm90^\circ\)

  • Every Euler convention has exactly two singularities — no exceptions.
  • For ZYX they sit at the second rotation: pitch \(\theta=\pm90^\circ\).
  • There the yaw and roll axes align — one rotational DOF is lost, and the Euler-rate map blows up.
  • The map \(\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}=W(\phi,\theta)\,\boldsymbol\omega\) has \(\cos\theta\) in a denominator ⇒ \(\to\infty\).
Why we care: an aerobatic quad will pitch through \(90^\circ\). Euler angles silently break there. That single fact is the entire motivation for the next slide — quaternions.
The singularity is at theta = ±90°, the SECOND (pitch) rotation — students often misremember it as roll or yaw. Tie it to euler_rate_matrix in dynamics.py: the 1/cos(theta) terms diverge at ±90°.

A third parametrisation

Axis–angle and Rodrigues' formula

Euler's rotation theorem (1775). Any orientation of a rigid body with one fixed point is a single rotation about a single axis. So three numbers suffice as \( \mathbf r = \alpha\,\mathbf u \): direction = axis, length = angle.

Rodrigues turns that description into a matrix without trigonometric bookkeeping:

\[ R = I + \sin\alpha\, \widehat{\mathbf u} + (1-\cos\alpha)\, \widehat{\mathbf u}^{\,2}, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf u} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -u_3 & u_2 \\ u_3 & 0 & -u_1 \\ -u_2 & u_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • \( \widehat{\mathbf u} \) is the skew (hat) operator: \( \widehat{\mathbf a}\mathbf b = \mathbf a \times \mathbf b \). Cross products become matrix products — a notation that will pay off next week.
  • Small \( \alpha \): \( R \approx I + \widehat{\mathbf r} \) — the same skew-symmetric first order we met two slides ago.
Why anyone bothers. Attitude error is naturally axis–angle: 'you are 12° off, about this axis'. Geometric attitude controllers (and the Mellinger controller inside gym-pybullet-drones) build their error term this way, precisely to avoid Euler singularities.
The hat operator is worth ten minutes because Wk 2's Euler equation and Wk 8's estimator both use it. Do the a-hat-b = a cross b check on the board.

Kinematics of rotation

How \( R \) changes: \( \dot R = R\,\widehat{\boldsymbol\omega} \)

1\( R^\top R = I \) holds for all time Start from the constraint. Orthonormality is not an approximation — it must survive differentiation.
2differentiate: \( \dot R^\top R + R^\top \dot R = 0 \) Product rule. So the matrix \( S = R^\top \dot R \) satisfies \( S^\top = -S \).
3\( S \) is skew-symmetric ⇒ \( S = \widehat{\boldsymbol\omega} \) for some vector \( \boldsymbol\omega \) The payoff. Skew-symmetric 3×3 matrices have exactly three free entries — and that vector is the angular velocity. Angular velocity is not defined into existence; it falls out of orthonormality.
4\( \dot R = R\,\widehat{\boldsymbol\omega} \) (with \( \boldsymbol\omega \) in body coordinates) Rearrange. This is what an attitude estimator integrates — and note it needs \( \boldsymbol\omega \), exactly what the gyroscope measures. No Euler angles anywhere: no singularity anywhere.

Next week we will connect this to Euler-angle rates through \( W(\boldsymbol\eta) \) — and see where the \( 1/\cos\theta \) comes from.

A genuinely beautiful four-step argument and entirely within reach. It also motivates why serious firmware integrates R or quaternions rather than angles.

The fix

Quaternions: singularity-free

A unit quaternion encodes the same orientation with no singularities anywhere. We use the scalar-FIRST (Hamilton) ordering:

\[ q = \begin{bmatrix} q_0 \\ q_1 \\ q_2 \\ q_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\tfrac{\vartheta}{2}) \\[2pt] \hat{\mathbf k}\,\sin(\tfrac{\vartheta}{2}) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lVert q \rVert = 1 \]
⚠ Pitfall: SciPy's Rotation.as_quat() returns scalar-LAST \([q_1,q_2,q_3,q_0]\) by default. Paste it into scalar-first code and your orientation is silently wrong. Always state the order; in recent SciPy pass scalar_first=True.
\(\hat{k}\) is the unit rotation axis, \(\vartheta\) the rotation angle. Rule of thumb: name the order on every slide and in every docstring. The scalar-last paste bug is one of the most common silent errors students hit later in the course — flag it hard now.

One level deeper

Quaternions, precisely enough to use

Definition (unit quaternion as rotation). \( q = \big(\cos\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\; \mathbf{u}\sin\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\big) \in \mathbb{S}^3 \) encodes a rotation by angle \( \alpha \) about unit axis \( \mathbf u \); vectors rotate by \( \mathbf v' = q\,\mathbf v\,q^{-1} \), and composition is quaternion multiplication (4 numbers, ~16 flops, no trig).
  • No singularity: the map covers every attitude — the price of a 4th number, per the 9−6=3 argument.
  • Double cover: \( q \) and \( -q \) are the same rotation. Naively averaging or interpolating quaternions across that seam is a classic firmware bug — normalise and take the shortest arc.
  • Who uses what: quadsim integrates Euler angles (transparent for teaching, fine near hover); PX4/Betaflight — and your team aircraft's firmware — run quaternions or rotation matrices internally, then display Euler angles for humans. Know both dialects; convert at the boundary.
Remark. For every controller in this course the attitude error is what matters, and near hover the Euler error ≈ the axis-angle error. The parametrisation choice is an engineering trade (transparency vs global validity), not a moral one.
Keep to 4 minutes. The double-cover bullet is the one exam-worthy subtlety; the "dialects" bullet is what they'll actually hit when reading firmware in Wk 5.

Concept check

Predict before you compute

Q1 — Roll 90° then pitch 90°, versus pitch 90° then roll 90°. Same final attitude?

No. Rotations do not commute — matrix order matters, which is exactly why we fix the ZYX convention on day one and never argue about it again. Try it with your phone.

Q2 — Why does the course simulator integrate body rates ω and not Euler-angle rates directly?

The gyro measures ω, and ω has no singularity — Euler rates blow up at θ=±90° (you just watched it). Physics lives in ω; Euler angles are a display format. Wk 2 makes this precise with the W(η) matrix.

Worked problem 1 · do it with me

Build \( R \) and check it

Given \( \phi = 30^\circ \), \( \theta = 0 \), \( \psi = 0 \). Build \( R \), then verify.

Step 1 — build. Only roll is non-zero, so \( R = R_x(30^\circ) \): \[ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.866 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0.866 \end{bmatrix} \]
Step 2 — three checks. (a) Columns unit length: \( 0.866^2 + 0.5^2 = 1 \) ✓
(b) Columns orthogonal: \( 0(0) + 0.866(-0.5) + 0.5(0.866) = 0 \) ✓
(c) \( \det R = +1 \) ✓ (not a reflection)
Step 3 — interpret. Third column \( = (0, -0.5, 0.866)^\top \): thrust now leans towards world \( -y \) with 86.6% of its magnitude still vertical. So a 30° roll costs 13% of your lift and buys \( 0.5g \approx 4.9 \) m/s² of sideways acceleration. That trade is the entire physics of quadrotor translation.
Do all three checks aloud; they become the assertion set students put in their Lab-1 code. The 13% lift loss number surprises people and sticks.

Worked problem 2 · you drive

From body measurement to world statement

Your aircraft is at \( \phi = 0 \), \( \theta = 20^\circ \), \( \psi = 45^\circ \), producing total thrust \( T = 6.4 \) N with mass \( 0.65 \) kg. Find the world-frame acceleration.

  1. Third column of \( R \): \( (c_\psi s_\theta,\; s_\psi s_\theta,\; c_\theta)^\top = (0.242,\; 0.242,\; 0.940)^\top \).
  2. Thrust in world: \( T \times \) that \( = (1.55,\; 1.55,\; 6.02) \) N.
  3. Add gravity: \( -mg\mathbf e_3 = (0,0,-6.38) \) N.
  4. Divide by \( m \): \( \mathbf a = (2.38,\; 2.38,\; -0.55) \) m/s².
Read the answer. It accelerates north-east at \( 3.4 \) m/s² and sinks at \( 0.55 \) m/s². The pitch bought horizontal acceleration but stole vertical thrust — so a pure attitude command always disturbs altitude unless thrust is raised to compensate.

That compensation, \( T = mg/(\cos\phi\cos\theta) \), is a real line in real autopilots and appears in your Week 6 controller.
(60 s) — What thrust would hold altitude exactly at this attitude? (\( T = 6.38/0.940 = 6.79 \) N — about 6% more.)
Let the room compute step 4 before revealing. The 'attitude disturbs altitude' insight is the motivation for Wk-6's thrust compensation.

Tie it to the sim

Check \(R\) with three lines of Python

import numpy as np
from quadsim.dynamics import rotation_matrix

R = rotation_matrix(phi=0.3, theta=-0.2, psi=1.1)   # ZYX, radians
print(np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)))               # True  -> orthonormal
print(round(np.linalg.det(R), 6))                    # 1.0   -> proper rotation
Two checks, two SO(3) laws: \(R^{\top}R=I\) and \(\det R=+1\). If either fails, you built the wrong matrix (often a frame- or order-mismatch). This is the warm-up for today's lab.

Before you leave

Four traps that cost real marks (and real airframes)

The trapThe truth
"Rotations add: roll 90° then pitch 90° = pitch then roll."Rotations compose, they don't commute. Fix the order (ZYX) once, state it in every derivation.
"The gyro measures \( \dot\phi, \dot\theta, \dot\psi \)."It measures body rates \( (p,q,r) \). The two differ by \( W(\boldsymbol\eta) \) — Week 2 makes it exact; confusing them breaks every estimator you'll write.
"Gimbal lock means the drone gets stuck."The aircraft is fine; the Euler chart fails. R and quaternions sail through 90° pitch untroubled.
"z-up vs z-down is cosmetic."It flips the sign of gravity, thrust and half the trig. Declare the frame in line 1 of every derivation — we grade for it.
Frame these as marks: each row has appeared as an exam distractor or a lab bug in real courses. The W(η) row seeds Week 2.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  • World is ENU / z-up (gravity \(-z\), thrust \(+\)body-\(z\)); \(R\) maps body→world.
  • \(R\in SO(3)\): \(R^{\top}R=I\), \(\det R=+1\). Built as ZYX \(R_z(\psi)R_y(\theta)R_x(\phi)\).
  • Euler has two singularities; for ZYX they're at pitch \(\pm90^\circ\) — hence quaternions.
  • Quaternions are scalar-first here; beware SciPy's scalar-last default.
