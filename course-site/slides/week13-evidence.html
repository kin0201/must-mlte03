Recap

Where we are, and what today adds

  • Week 11 gave you the instrument: a written specification, requirement IDs, and certify.py — PASS or FAIL against a fixed bar, the same bar for every student, on a vehicle that is yours alone.
  • Week 12 gave you the method: the finite-horizon OCP, the QP, the hard/soft split, and a controller that plans instead of reacting.
  • Today is neither. Today is the argument: how you turn two certification reports into a claim an examiner has to accept.
Why this is a whole week. Because a controller that works is worth nothing you can defend until somebody else can reproduce the reason it works.

Every year, students arrive at the viva with a green report and no ability to say why the number moved. They lose marks not for the engineering but for the evidence — which is the cheaper half to get right.

Certification is binary. Your claim is comparative. Today is entirely about the second one.

Sixty seconds of orientation; the instrument / method / argument split frames the whole session.

Orientation

By the end of today you can…

  1. State the five conditions that make a comparison controlled, and check your own harness against them.
  2. Explain why a fully deterministic controller produces a different verdict on a different seed — and quantify it.
  3. Report a result as a sample, not a fact: graded number, spread, worst case, in one honest sentence.
  4. Perform a requirements review — decide whether a requirement is verifiable at all before writing a test for it.
  5. Write a failure up so that it scores.
Definition (controlled comparison). A comparison in which exactly one thing differs between the two runs — the controller — and every other condition (mission, vehicle, disturbance sequence, limits, metric code, duration, start state) is provably identical.

Provably, not "we think so": one shared config object, one metric module, seeds written down, one student ID on both runs.
Read the definition aloud slowly — 'exactly one thing differs' is the entire week compressed.

Opening quiz · 00:00

Two from last week, before we build on them

Q1 — Your MPC and your cascade PID give nearly identical trajectories on your mission. Is the MPC broken?

Almost certainly not — check the activation plot first. Unconstrained, linear, quadratic ⇒ MPC reduces to LQR-like behaviour; they should coincide when no constraint is active. "MPC ≈ PID" is a statement about the mission, not about your code. What it does mean is that your report cannot claim the constraint handling mattered — so either harden the mission or make the claim humbler.

Q2 — On M3 the mass steps from 0.65 to 0.45 kg at \( t = 5 \) s and the controller is never told. Name the mechanism by which MPC recovers faster than the PID.

It rations thrust over the horizon instead of reacting after the error has grown. The optimiser plans \( N \) steps of input against the measured state; when the aircraft starts climbing, the whole plan is rebuilt from the new state and the correction is distributed across the horizon rather than delivered as one clipped burst. That sentence — with your own numbers in it — is item 3 of your report.

Q2's answer is literally the paragraph they must write; say that out loud.

Block A · 00:10

The sentence your report has to earn

The claim. "My frozen baseline fails REQ-M3-1 at 0.540 m against a 0.50 m bar. My MPC submission certifies at 0.473 m on the same mission, same seed, same vehicle, same harness — because the planner rations the post-drop thrust correction over a 1 s horizon instead of delivering it after the error has grown."

Five parts: a number that failed · a number that passed · proof they are comparable · a mechanism — and it is your claim about your aircraft. Miss any one and the sentence stops being evidence.

0.540 / 0.473 are the course-default vehicle. Substitute your own two numbers, from --student <your ID>.

Which part do students miss? Never the first two — certify.py hands those to you.

Part three is missed silently: the two runs differed in something nobody wrote down.

Part four is missed loudly: "it got better" or "MPC is more advanced". That is a vibe, not a mechanism.

Blocks A and B fix part three. Block D fixes part four.
Everything in the session hangs off this slide; return to it at the close.

What makes a comparison controlled — five conditions

Row 5 is new this year and it is the one that will bite. Rows 2 and 3 get broken by accident; row 5 gets broken by helpfulness — a classmate hands over a baseline number and it looks like a favour.

Block A · condition 5 · new this year

Your classmate's baseline is not your baseline

  • Every student's vehicle differs: mass, arm length, \( k_\tau \), inertia — all derived from the student ID, all fixed forever for that ID.
  • So "same vehicle" joins "same seed", "same script" and "frozen baseline" as a condition of a controlled comparison. It is not an extra rule; it is the same rule, on a quantity that used to be constant.
  • Comparing your MPC's number against a classmate's baseline number is not a weak comparison. It is not a comparison at all — two things differ, and one of them is the aircraft.
The mechanical check: both certification reports print the student ID. Open both, read the two IDs, confirm they are yours. Ten seconds; it closes the whole failure mode.
What you can share, and should. Theory. Derivations. Why a slack penalty is computed rather than searched. What a solver error means. Reading each other's code is allowed and encouraged — this is a course, not a sealed exam.

What does not transfer is the result. A horizon and a weight matrix tuned against my aircraft do not certify yours; you have to make the decision for your own numbers, which is precisely the thing being examined.
And the flip side. Nobody can tell you your number is wrong by comparing it to theirs. The only instrument that can judge your controller is certify.py on your own ID — which is a cleaner position to be in than it sounds.
Expect at least one student to have already borrowed a classmate's baseline figure. Say the "not a weak comparison — not a comparison at all" line slowly.

Block A · the analogy that already worked

One plant — you have done this before, in Week 10

The same discipline, in software. Week 10 was already a controlled comparison: same controller, same estimator, same seed, two plants — and everything in the difference was the plant, because nothing else moved. certify.py is that discipline made permanent: the mission function is the arena, the seed is the wind on the day, your student ID is the aircraft you walked in with.

In software you can hold the wind fixed — so if you do not, you have thrown away an advantage the physical world never offers.
Why the Week-10 analogy is worth the slide. Because it makes the failure mode obvious. Nobody in a debrief would have accepted "our realistic column is better because we changed kp_pos between the two flights" — that is exactly the undocumented change the workshop forbade. In the project the equivalent happens constantly and invisibly, because changing the conditions costs one edited line, and changing the aircraft costs one forgotten --student.
Five conditions, one sentence each: same mission · same estimator · same seed(s) · same vehicle (--student) · the baseline frozen at a git hash. Week 10 gave you four of the five for free; the fifth is this week's first job.
Point back at the two-column table from the workshop — the physical version of a shared config object.

Block A · condition 2

The frozen baseline — what freezing actually means

  1. Run your Week-9 classical controller on M3, through certify.py, with --student <your ID>, from estimated state.
  2. Save the report verbatim — the text, not a summary of it — as baseline-M3.txt.
  3. Record alongside it: student ID, git hash, aircraft params, seed, date, and the exact command line.
  4. Commit the controller and the report together. Tag it baseline-v1.
  5. Paste the report into the report skeleton now. It is item (a); it is already finished.
Frozen means "changes leave a trace". Not "we promise not to touch it". A baseline that improves quietly between Week 12 and Week 15 turns your central claim into self-marking homework — and the viva finds it in the first question, because the numbers in the report will not reproduce.

If you genuinely must re-run the baseline (a bug fix, not a tuning improvement), say so in the report, in one sentence, with both numbers. That is a stronger paper than a silent edit.

This was Week 12's gate. If you have not done it, it is the first thing you do in the lab hour — before any MPC work.

Ask for a show of hands on who has baseline-v1 tagged; chase the ones who do not during the lab.

Block A · condition 4

The unfair comparison that happens by accident

A true story, most years. A student gives the MPC a tilt limit of 20° — because it handles the limit gracefully and the constraint is expressible inside the optimiser. The baseline keeps 12°, because that is what it was tuned with in Week 9.

The MPC "wins" by 40%.

Nobody intended to cheat. But the comparison now measures the clamp, not the method — and an examiner who asks "what tilt limit did the baseline have?" collapses the whole result in one question.
The fix is mechanical, not moral. Write the harness so that swapping controllers is a one-line change: one config object, one mission call, one metric module, and the controller passed in as an argument.

If it is harder than one line, the comparison will drift — and drift always flatters whichever controller you worked on last. That is not cynicism; it is just where attention goes.

The course harness already enforces this: certify.py --controller module:Class --student <your ID> --mission M3 — the controller is one argument, the vehicle is another, and both runs carry the same ID. Use it for the baseline too. Every hand-rolled comparison script is a chance to break condition 4 or 5.

'Drift always flatters whichever controller you worked on last' — say it slowly; it lands.

Block A · write it down

The evaluation protocol, as a paragraph in your report

  1. Same mission function, same start state, same duration — named, with its parameters.
  2. Same vehicle — --student <your ID> on both runs; the ID stated in the report.
  3. Same limits — the baseline gets the identical tilt and thrust clamps.
  4. Same estimator — both controllers close the loop on estimated state (the Week-8 rule: no ground-truth shortcut).
  5. Same seeds, listed explicitly in the report.
  6. Same metric code — imported, never re-implemented.
  7. Both raw reports kept, pasted verbatim, not summarised.
What it reads like, finished. "Both controllers were run through certify.py on mission M3 (hover at [0,0,1.5]; the mass step at t = 5 s; 15 s), from estimated state, on the vehicle derived from student ID <my ID> (mass ___ → ___ kg, arm ___ m) with estimator seed 0, using the requirement thresholds as implemented in the harness. Only the --controller argument differed. Both reports are reproduced in full in §2."

Five lines. Written once. It closes the entire family of "but was it fair?" questions.

This paragraph is worth more marks per minute of writing than anything else in the report, because it is the paragraph that makes the other numbers mean something.

Tell them to draft this paragraph during the lab hour while the details are in front of them.

Concept check · 00:40

Four comparisons — which ones survive?

Q1 — Student A reports: baseline RMSE 0.46 m (from their Week-9 notes), MPC RMSE 0.26 m (run today). Verdict?

Not a comparison. The baseline number predates a fortnight of changes to the simulator, the estimator wrapper and possibly the metric code. Re-run the baseline today, through the same harness. It costs one command and it is the difference between evidence and an anecdote.

Q2 — Student B ran both controllers today, same harness, same command — but did not record the seed. Verdict?

Salvageable, but currently unverifiable. If both ran with the harness default (seed 0) it is fine — but nobody can confirm that from the report. Re-run with --seed 0 stated explicitly. Block B shows the verdict itself can flip on the seed.

Q3 — Student C's MPC beats the baseline by 3%. Both runs are impeccably controlled. Is that a result?

It is an honest result, and it may not be a significant one. 3% is well inside the across-seed spread you are about to see. Do not hide it or inflate it — say so: "the improvement is 3% on seed 0, smaller than this mission's seed-to-seed variation; I do not claim a robust improvement." That sentence scores. Claiming a 3% victory does not.

Q4 — Student D reports her own MPC number, and the baseline number her classmate measured. Verdict?

Not a comparison at all — and the fix is one command. Two things differ: the controller and the aircraft. The classmate's baseline was measured on a different mass, arm and inertia, so the gap is partly her MPC and partly two different quadcopters. Re-run the frozen baseline with --student <her own ID>. Condition 5 — sixty seconds to satisfy.

Q3 sets up Block B; Q4 is the new one and the one to dwell on. Let each answer hang for a moment before advancing.

Block B · 00:50

Your controller does not fly on the truth

  • Every mission in certify.py closes the loop on the estimated state — EstimatedStateController wraps your submission.
  • That estimate comes from simulated sensors, driven by a seeded noise generator.
  • Change the seed and you change the sensor-noise sequence — and therefore the estimate, and therefore every command your controller issued, from the first millisecond onward.
  • The disturbance history is part of the experimental condition. It is not a cosmetic knob.
The consequence, stated plainly. One run is one sample.

Not a measurement of your controller. A measurement of your controller on one draw of the noise.

Everything in this block follows from that single sentence.

Week 8's estimator, back for one last appearance — this time not as a design problem but as the reason your results have variance.

This is the causal chain: seed → noise → estimate → command → trajectory → verdict. Walk it once, slowly.

Block B

"But my controller has no randomness in it"

Correct — and irrelevant. A cascade PID is fully deterministic given its input. Feed it the same numbers and it produces the same numbers, every time, forever.

But it is not fed the same numbers. It is fed an estimate, and the estimate is a function of the seed. Determinism of the controller says nothing whatever about determinism of the closed loop.
Why this matters more than it sounds. The usual student intuition is that run-to-run variance is a problem of stochastic methods — learned controllers, sampling-based planners — and that a classical controller is exempt.

The next slide is the course's own reference CascadePID. No learning. No sampling. No randomness anywhere in the controller. Watch what happens.

Read the numbers on the next slide before you decide how many seeds your own report needs.

Deliberately set up the surprise; do not preview the numbers.

Block B · read the table properly

Three lessons and one warning

  1. The verdict flips. Seed 1 misses the bar by 44%; seed 2 passes with room to spare. Anyone reporting a single run has reported a coin toss.
  2. The spread is 3.4×. 0.211 m to 0.721 m, from a controller with no randomness in it at all. A controller is characterised by a distribution, not by a number.
  3. The graded seed is not the typical seed. Seed 0 (0.540 m) sits in the worst quartile of the eight. That is luck, not design — and it cuts both ways.
The warning. Do not read this as "the grading is unfair, so the number does not matter".

Every student is graded on seed 0, on the same mission, against the same three bars, with the same tool. The instrument is identical for everyone; only the aircraft is yours — which is why you compare your number to your own baseline and to nobody else's.

What the table forbids is a different thing: claiming more than one run can support. You are graded on seed 0. You are expected to know, and to say, what the other seeds did.

And notice the sharpest consequence: a student could "fix" nothing, re-roll the seed, and produce a green report. That is why the seed is fixed at 0 — and why silently choosing a favourable seed, or a favourable student ID, is academic misconduct rather than clever engineering.

Say the misconduct sentence plainly once. It removes the temptation before it forms.

Block B · why, mechanically

Where a 3.4× spread comes from, in a deterministic loop

1The estimator's position error is a random process; at \( t = 5^- \) s the state estimate is off by some \( \boldsymbol\varepsilon \) that depends on the seed. The initial condition of the recovery is random. Not the controller — the situation it starts from.
2The mass steps 0.65 → 0.45 kg. Thrust is unchanged for one sample, so the aircraft is suddenly over-thrusted by \( (0.65-0.45)\,g \approx 2 \) N and begins to climb. The disturbance itself is deterministic — the same 2 N every time. It is not the disturbance that varies.
3The controller responds to \( \hat{\mathbf x} \), not \( \mathbf x \). The correction it commands is driven by an error signal that already carries \( \boldsymbol\varepsilon \). A favourable \( \boldsymbol\varepsilon \) makes the loop react early; an unfavourable one delays it by a few samples. A few samples of 2 N of unopposed force is centimetres, and it compounds.
4Peak deviation is a maximum over the run — an extreme-value statistic, not an average. This is the amplifier. RMSE over 10 s averages the noise out; a maximum picks out the worst single instant, so it inherits the tail of the distribution and varies far more. Look at REQ-M3-3 (tail RMSE): 0.132–0.287 m — a 2.2× spread, visibly tighter than REQ-M3-1's 3.4×.

Design consequence: a requirement written on a maximum will always be noisier than one written on a mean. That is a fact about the metric, and it belongs in your discussion section.

Step 4 is the intellectual payoff — max-statistics inherit tails. Compare the two spreads on the previous slide's data.

Block B · worked example

The honest sentence — three versions, one of them scores

VersionWhat the examiner reads
"My MPC achieves 0.47 m and certifies M3."A single sample presented as a property of the controller. Nothing about it is wrong; everything about it is incomplete.
"My MPC achieves 0.47 m and certifies M3. Results vary with the seed."Aware, but unquantified — the reader cannot tell whether "vary" means 2% or 200%. A hedge, not a measurement.
"My MPC certifies M3 on the graded seed 0 at 0.473 m against the 0.50 m bar. Across seeds 0–2 the max deviation ranges 0.41–0.52 m, so the margin on seed 0 is smaller than the seed-to-seed variation: I claim a certified result on the graded seed, not a robust one. The baseline fails on seeds 0 and 1 and passes on 2."This scores. It reports the graded number, quantifies the spread, states exactly what is and is not being claimed, and gives the baseline the same treatment.

Note what version 3 does not do: apologise, inflate, or hide behind statistics. Three sentences, all of them checkable.

The illustrative 0.41–0.52 range is a template, not a result — students substitute their own.

Block B · the practical question

How many seeds, and what to report from them

  1. Three is the floor for the improvement-over-baseline claim, and it is what the spec asks for. Eight is better and costs a coffee break.
  2. Report min, max and the graded value. Those three numbers carry almost all the information for \( n \le 8 \).
  3. Report a mean only with the range beside it. A mean alone re-hides exactly what the sweep revealed.
  4. Do not compute a p-value. With \( n = 3 \) it is theatre, and an examiner who knows statistics will say so.
  5. Use the same seed set for baseline and submission. Otherwise you have re-broken condition 3 while trying to satisfy it.
The standard you are being held to. Not "prove it statistically". You have \( n = 3 \) and no time; nobody expects a confidence interval.

The standard is do not claim more than your data supports, and make it possible for the reader to check. That is achievable in one sentence and it is what professional engineering reports actually do.

A claim that is careful is worth more than a claim that is large.
Point 4 pre-empts the student who will try to impress with a t-test on three samples.

Your turn · 3 minutes

Judge four claims

(3 min, in pairs) — For each claim: acceptable, overclaimed, or not a comparison at all?
  1. "MPC reduced max deviation from 0.540 m to 0.473 m on seed 0, certifying REQ-M3-1."
  2. "MPC is 12% better than PID."
  3. "MPC certified on seeds 0, 1 and 2; the baseline failed on 0 and 1. The improvement holds across every seed I tested."
  4. "MPC certified on seed 4, where the baseline also certifies, so I used seed 4 for the report."

1 — acceptable (specific, single-seed, correctly scoped).   2 — overclaimed (better at what? on what? "12%" of a max-statistic with a 3.4× spread is noise dressed as a result).   3 — acceptable and strong (quantified, same seed set for both, claim scoped to "every seed I tested").   4 — not a comparison; it is seed-shopping — and reporting on a non-graded seed chosen because it was favourable is misconduct. If seed 4 is interesting, report it in addition to seed 0, and say why.

Item 4 will get an uncomfortable laugh. Good — that is the one worth remembering.

Block C · 01:15

A requirement can itself be defective

Definition (requirements review). The step that checks a requirement is verifiable at all — unambiguous, measurable, and achievable by something — before anyone writes a test for it.

It comes before verification, not after. Testing an unverifiable requirement produces a confident FAIL that means nothing.
  • Week 11 taught you to read a requirement and check a controller against it.
  • That is only half of certification. The other half is reading the requirement and checking it.
  • A requirement is defective if it is unmeasurable, self-contradictory, ambiguous, or — the interesting case — demands more than the system can physically deliver.
  • The course harness contains a real, unplanned example of the last kind. We are going to work it.
'Testing an unverifiable requirement produces a confident FAIL that means nothing' — the line to remember.

Block C · real measured data

Worked example — REQ-M2-1 asks for the impossible

REQ-M2-1: tracking RMSE over two periods of the figure-eight < 0.15 m.
The loop closes on the estimated state. So the question nobody asked when the bar was set:
how accurately can the aircraft perceive its own position on that trajectory?

M2 tracking RMSEvs the 0.15 m bar

Read the first row again. 0.147 m against a 0.15 m bar.

A controller that tracked its own estimate perfectly — zero control error, a physical impossibility — would still miss the truth by 0.147 m RMSE, and would pass the requirement by 3 mm.

Any real controller, with any control error at all, fails. The requirement asks students to fly more accurately than they can perceive.

This is why M1 and M2 are demonstration missions, not project missions. They are here to be reviewed, not to be passed.

Pause on '0.147 against 0.15'. The three-millimetre margin does the teaching by itself.

Block C

Whose defect is it?

Not the controller's. Every controller fails, including ones with nothing wrong with them. A test that no correct implementation can pass is not measuring implementations.
Not the estimator's, either. 0.147 m is what that sensor suite, at that noise level, on that trajectory, supports. You could tighten it — better sensors, a better filter — but that is a different design change, and the requirement never said which subsystem was supposed to deliver.
It is the requirement's. It was written as a control requirement and silently levied on the whole sensing-and-control chain. The moment the loop closes on an estimate, tracking accuracy is bounded below by estimation accuracy — and the bar was set without checking that bound.

The correct engineering response is not to try harder. It is to raise the finding, and then either relax the bar above the noise floor, or change the verification method to measure control error against the estimate rather than the truth.

Viva question B8 asks you to make this argument yourself, in your own words, with these numbers. Rehearse it once today and it is free marks in Week 15.

The 'relax the bar or change the verification method' pair is the professional answer; both are legitimate.

Block C · how it was found

Documented, not quietly deleted

  • Found on 2026-08-25, while adding the --seed flag to the harness.
  • The bars for M1 and M2 had been set so that the classical baseline would fail them — which it does, reliably.
  • They were never checked against what any controller could achieve on estimated state. The check that was missing is exactly the one this block is teaching.
  • The requirements were left in the harness, marked demonstration only, with the finding written up.
Why leave a broken requirement in? Because deleting it would have deleted the lesson. A specification with a documented defect and a written rationale is a more truthful artifact than a specification that was silently corrected — and it is what a real project's requirements database looks like after review.

There is a professional habit hiding in here: when you find that a requirement cannot be met, the deliverable is the finding, not the excuse. Raise it early, in writing, with numbers.

Your project inherits the same standard. If you discover a requirement in your own work that cannot be met, say so, with the number that proves it — that is a result, and it is graded as one.

This slide is where 'a well-diagnosed failure beats a silently-omitted one' first appears; it returns in Block D.

Block D · the one you owe

Item (c) — one figure, one paragraph, one mechanism

  1. Plot the altitude trace through the mass drop, baseline and submission on the same axes, same seed.
  2. Mark \( t = 5 \) s. Mark the 0.50 m bar as a dashed line.
  3. Annotate the two peaks with their numbers — the same numbers as in the certification reports.
  4. Caption it with the claim, not with a description of the axes.
  5. Underneath: one paragraph naming the feature of MPC responsible.
The paragraph, done badly and well. Badly: "The MPC performs better after the drop." Describes the figure. Adds nothing.

Well: "The planner solves for the next 1.0 s of thrust against the measured state, so from the first sample after the step it distributes the correction across the horizon rather than delivering it as a single clipped burst. The baseline's correction is proportional to an error that must first grow — visible as the 0.067 m higher peak, 0.3 s later."

Notice what the good paragraph contains: a mechanism, a timing, and a number lifted straight from the plot. That is the shape of every explanatory paragraph you will ever write as an engineer.

Have them draft this paragraph in the lab hour, even before the figure is pretty.

Block D · optional craft

The constraint-activation plot, and how to draw it

  1. Plot each constrained quantity against time, with its bound drawn as a dashed line.
  2. Shade the intervals where the constraint is active — within a small tolerance of the bound, or slack \( s_k > 0 \).
  3. Overlay the baseline's trace for the same quantity, on the same axes.
  4. Caption it with the count: "MPC: 0 violations; clamped baseline: 2 violations, max 0.12 m outside."
What the figure has to show — two things at once. (1) The constraint mattered — shaded regions exist.
(2) Your method respected it while the baseline did not.

Both, or the figure is decoration. One shaded region and identical behaviour inside it means the constraint is not binding hard enough to distinguish the controllers.

Cost: about forty minutes if you logged the slack values. Several hours if you did not — because you have to re-run every experiment. Log first, plot later.

The logging warning is the practical takeaway; it lands better here than on the logging slide.

Block D · the awkward result

"Nothing was shaded" — what that actually means

It is not a bug. It is a finding. No constraint activated ⇒ the optimiser never had to trade anything off ⇒ your MPC was solving an unconstrained problem ⇒ it should look like LQR, and it does.

Your code is fine. Your claim is what has to change.
Three legitimate responses, in order of preference. 1. Make the claim honest. "On M3 at my tuning, no constraint activated; the improvement therefore comes from preview and horizon, not from constraint handling." Precise, defensible, and it names the real mechanism.

2. Harden the mission — a tighter corridor, a faster tempo — and report both settings.

3. Say nothing about constraints at all and let the mechanism figure carry the argument. Entirely acceptable: constraints are not the only thing MPC brings.

What is not acceptable is the fourth response — claiming the constraint handling helped, with no activation anywhere in the log. That is the single easiest claim for a viva to dismantle, because the check takes ten seconds.

Response 1 is the one most students need. Make it sound like the strong option, because it is.

Block D · optional craft

The anticipation figure — proving it planned rather than reacted

The receding horizon, from Week 12. The anticipation figure is this picture drawn from your log: the faint predicted trajectory, overlaid on what was actually executed.

How to draw it. Pick two or three decisive moments — typically just before a constraint activates, or the sample right after the mass step. At each, plot the full predicted horizon as a faint dashed line on top of the executed trajectory.

Three ghosts on one plot. More is clutter.
What it proves. That the controller saw it coming — the visual that distinguishes MPC from every reactive method.

Cheap, if you stored the predicted trajectory. Store it sparsely: every 5th step of every 10th solve is plenty for the figure and keeps the log a sane size.
The sparse-storage tip prevents the multi-gigabyte log that one student produces every year.

Block D · the intellectual content

Necessary versus sufficient

The distinction. A working controller proves your parameters are sufficient.

An ablation proves they are necessary.

Only the second one is an argument about design. The first is an argument about luck.
What it sounds like in a viva. "I chose \( N = 25 \)." → invites "why?", and you are now improvising.

"I chose \( N = 25 \); at \( N = 8 \) the anticipation vanishes and the behaviour matches the baseline, as my critical-horizon calculation predicted." → ends the question.

Same controller. Same marks available. One of them earned them.

This is why ablation is the most persuasive evidence in engineering generally, not just here: it is the only technique that shows a choice mattered rather than merely worked.

The necessary/sufficient framing is the transferable idea; say it once cleanly.

Block D · optional craft

The timing histogram — real time as a deadline argument

  1. State the deadline. 25 Hz ⇒ 40 ms, of which the solver may have, say, 25 ms.
  2. Log every solve time for a full flight — the distribution, not the mean.
  3. Histogram it, with the deadline drawn as a vertical line.
  4. Report mean, p95, p99, and the number of missed deadlines.
  5. State the miss policy: what the controller did on a miss, and how often it fired.
Why the mean is nearly useless here. Optimisation solve times are long-tailed. A distribution with a 9 ms mean can easily contain 50 ms outliers when the active constraint set changes.

One 50 ms outlier at 25 Hz is a dropped control step. The mean cannot see it; the histogram cannot hide it.

A deadline is a p95/p99 contract, not an average.

Your project is simulation-only, so nothing here is graded. It is on the slide because "would this run on the aircraft?" is a standard viva question, and a histogram answers it in one image.

Note explicitly that this is a viva-preparation slide, not a deliverable.

Block D · the part nobody teaches

How to write a failure up so that it scores

  1. State the failure first, with its requirement ID and its number. No burying it in paragraph four.
  2. Show the evidence you used to diagnose it — the trace, the log line, the sweep.
  3. Name the mechanism, or name honestly what you could not determine.
  4. Say what you tried and what each attempt changed — including the attempts that did nothing.
  5. Say what you would do next, specifically enough that someone could do it.
The rule, stated as the marks see it. A well-diagnosed failure beats a silently-omitted one — every time, and by a wide margin.

An omitted failure costs you the marks for the result and the marks for the analysis, and it usually surfaces in the viva anyway, where it now also costs credibility.

A diagnosed failure keeps the analysis marks in full. The scheme is built this way deliberately — the fallback rule caps only the four artifact items (certification · improvement · report · code = 60 marks) at 45/60. Your viva (20), engineering log (10) and quizzes (10) are untouched.

"I could not certify REQ-M3-1; here is exactly why, here is what I ruled out, here is what I would try next" is a report a professional engineer would sign. "Certified" with a quietly deleted run is not.

Say the cap explicitly: no faking, no despair. It is what keeps incentives honest.
