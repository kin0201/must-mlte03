The two questions

“Does it work?” is not the question

Does it work?
You ran it. It looked fine. You believe it.
Evidence: your memory, and everyone who was watching.
Is it certified?
It was run against a written specification, by a procedure anyone can repeat, and every requirement has a recorded pass/fail with evidence attached.

Real flight software is never accepted on the first kind of answer. Not once, anywhere, for any aircraft that carries people.

Let this sit. Ask: which one did Week 9's 0.226 m give us? Answer: the left column, once, on one plant.

Block A · the standard

A demonstration is one observation

Suppose the Week-10 workshop had let you take three runs with three seeds and keep the best. Consider what that means as evidence.

The arithmetic of a lucky run. Suppose your controller clears a threshold on any given seed with probability \(p = 0.5\) — a coin flip. The chance that at least one of three runs looks perfect is \(1-0.5^3 = 87.5\%\). You would almost certainly have gone home believing it works. (The eight-seed table in §6 of the spec is this arithmetic with real numbers: CascadePID fails REQ-M3-1 on two seeds of eight.)
A demonstration selects for luck. A certification is designed to not let you do that.
This is the hinge of the whole session. If they get this, everything else follows.

Block A · the chain

Requirement → verification method → evidence

Three artifacts, in a fixed order. Every safety-critical industry uses this shape.

ArtifactThe question it answersExample from your project
RequirementWhat must be true?REQ-M3-1: max deviation after the drop < 0.50 m
Verification methodHow do we check it?Test: run mission M3 in certify.py, seed 0
EvidenceWhat is the record that it held?[PASS] REQ-M3-1 max = 0.473 m

The links between them are called traceability. Every requirement traces to a method; every method produces evidence; nothing ships on “it worked when I ran it.”

Traceability is the single word that carries the discipline. Say it twice.

Block A · the four methods

You can verify four ways — a single run is none of them

MethodWhat it meansWhen it is the right choice
TestRun it, measure, compare to the thresholdAnything with a number. Your whole project.
AnalysisProve it mathematically or by modellingStability margins; worst cases you cannot fly.
InspectionRead the design or the codeCoding standards; a bound that is structural.
DemonstrationShow it working, qualitativelyLast resort. Weakest evidence of the four.
Note where the nominal-model run sat. At the bottom row. That is not a criticism of Weeks 4–9 — building the loop taught you things no lecture can. It is a statement about what one successful run can and cannot support; Week 10 showed you exactly how little.
DO-178C names exactly these four. Students often assume 'verification' means 'testing'; it does not.

Block A · where this comes from

The same shape in every safety-critical industry

StandardDomainWhat it governs
DO-178CCivil avionics softwareEverything flying in certified airspace
ISO 26262AutomotiveAnything that can steer or brake a car
IEC 62304Medical devicesSoftware in devices that touch patients
MIL-STD-882Defence systemsSystem safety across the lifecycle

Different words, same skeleton: written requirements, an assigned verification method per requirement, recorded evidence, and an auditable trace between them.

Why a drone course cares. Regulators are converging on exactly this for UAS. If you work on anything that flies, drives, or carries a patient, this is the vocabulary of your working life.
Do not go deep into any one standard. The point is the shape is universal, not the clause numbers.

Block A · writing one

What makes a requirement usable

  • Falsifiable — there is an outcome that would clearly break it.
  • Quantified — a number and a unit, not an adjective.
  • Bounded — it says over what window, under what conditions.
  • Verifiable — some method could actually check it, with the equipment you have.
  • Singular — one claim, so a failure points at one thing.
Requirement. A statement of what the system must do, written so that a competent stranger could check it and reach the same verdict you would.
The 'competent stranger' test is the practical one. If two engineers could disagree about the verdict, it is not a requirement yet.

Block A · the anti-pattern

Three requirements that cannot be verified

Written asWhat is wrong
“The controller shall be stable.”No condition, no window, no measurement. Stable under what?
“Tracking shall be accurate.”No number. Accurate is an opinion until it has a unit.
“The drone shall recover quickly from disturbances.”Two undefined terms and no threshold. Quickly? Which disturbances?
Each of these sounds like an engineering requirement and none can be tested. Writing them is the most common failure in a first specification.
Ask the room to fix one aloud before showing the exercise slide.

Concept check

Repair these three

On paper, in pairs. Rewrite each so it is falsifiable, quantified and bounded. You may invent reasonable numbers — but say what they are.

  1. “The controller shall be stable.”
  2. “Tracking shall be accurate.”
  3. “The drone shall recover quickly from disturbances.”
(10 min) — Rewrite all three, then swap with the pair beside you and try to break theirs.

A good repair of the third looks like REQ-M3-1, which you will meet in twenty minutes.

Circulate. The usual miss is forgetting the condition (under what wind? from what initial error?).

Block B · --student

You are each certifying a different aircraft

Your vehicle, from your student ID. The final project is individual. --student <your ID> builds your quadcopter before the mission runs: your mass (and your mass step), your arm length, your yaw-torque ratio \( k_\tau \), your inertia. It is required for the graded run.
# see your own numbers, before you design anything

# the graded command — the ID is not optional

Deterministic, not random. The same ID gives the same aircraft on any machine, forever — so your result reproduces exactly, and mine reproduces yours. A different ID never gives your aircraft.
The requirements do not change. Same three bars, same 0.50 m, same seed 0, for everyone. Only the airframe underneath differs — which is why the numbers on my slides are not your numbers.

Block B · the mission

M3 — the payload drop

The quadrotor holds position at \([0,\,0,\,1.5]\).

At \(t = 5\) s its mass steps down — a package released. (0.65 kg → 0.45 kg on the course default vehicle; your two masses come from your student ID.)

Your controller is never told. One continuous instance, whole flight. It has to notice through feedback, like a real flight computer.

Why this mission:

  • Physically obvious — everyone can picture it.
  • The failure is directional: too much thrust for the new mass ⇒ it climbs.
  • A fixed-gain loop does recover — just not tightly enough.
  • Small enough that the process is the hard part, not the control theory.
This is the ONLY project mission this term. Say so explicitly — there is no menu to choose from.

Block B · the spec

M3, written out

15 s total. Mass steps down at t = 5 s. Graded on estimator seed 0, on your own vehicle — --student <your ID>.

IDRequirementThreshold
REQ-M3-1Max deviation across the entire post-drop window< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-2Max transient deviation in the first 3 s after the drop< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-3Position RMSE over the last 5 s of the run< 0.50 m
Read them as a set. REQ-M3-2 asks how bad does it get immediately. REQ-M3-3 asks did it actually settle. REQ-M3-1 covers the whole window and catches anything the other two miss. Together they describe ‘recovered properly’ without ever using that phrase.
Point out that each requirement is singular — a failure tells you which aspect broke.

Block B · what that report says

It failed exactly one requirement

  • It did recover — REQ-M3-2 and REQ-M3-3 both pass comfortably.
  • It did not recover tightly enough — REQ-M3-1 misses by 0.04 m.
  • That is a diagnosis, not a verdict. You know precisely what to attack.
A good specification does not just tell you that you failed. It tells you which part failed, and by how much.
Compare. “It didn't work” vs “max deviation was 0.540 m against a 0.50 m bar, but the transient and the settle were both fine.” Only one of those sentences suggests what to do on Monday.
This slide is the argument for specifications, made concrete. Slow down here.

Block B · no shortcuts

The loop closes on the estimate

Every mission wraps your controller in EstimatedStateController. You get the same noisy IMU and low-rate GPS you fused in Week 8 — never the simulator's ground truth.

Your controller sees the output of the estimator, not the truth the simulator holds.

The Week-8 rule still applies. A controller that peeks at true state is not a flight controller, and it will not certify anything.
Ground-truth leakage is the most common integrity problem in submissions. Name it now, not in Week 14.

Block C · trap one

The seed is part of the experiment

Your controller does not fly on the truth — it flies on an estimate built from simulated noisy sensors, driven by a seeded random generator.

Change the seed and you change the exact noise history your controller had to survive.

A single run is a single sample.
This is not a machine-learning problem. There is nothing random inside CascadePID. It is fully deterministic given its input. The randomness is in the world, and that is enough.
Pre-empt the assumption that variance only afflicts learned controllers. Next slide kills it with data.

Block C · interactive

Block C · read it properly

Three lessons in one table

  1. The verdict flips. Seed 1 misses by 44%. Seed 2 passes with room to spare. One run is a coin toss reported as a result.
  2. The spread is 3.4×. 0.211 m to 0.721 m. A controller is characterised by its distribution, not its best day.
  3. The typical case hides the bad case. It passes on six of eight. Sample three at random and you would conclude it passes comfortably — and miss the tail that matters.
You are graded on seed 0 — one fixed draw, the same for everyone. --seeds 0,1,2 measures the spread for your report; it is not the grading rule. On this vehicle seed 0 is one of the two the baseline fails. Your report must say so.
Be honest that seed 0 is a harsh draw for the baseline. That honesty is itself the lesson.

Block C · what to do

Report the spread, not the best run

# graded — the one that counts, seed 0, your vehicle
python certify.py --controller mypkg:MyController --student <your ID> --mission M3

# and once, for your report — measures spread, does NOT change the grade
python certify.py --controller mypkg:MyController --student <your ID> --mission M3 --seeds 0,1,2
One sentence, worth more than a page of tuning narrative. “My margin on REQ-M3-1 is 0.47 m on the graded seed, but ranges 0.21–0.72 m across seeds 0–2, so I would not claim a robust pass.”

That sentence is required in your report. It is the honest version of the claim.

Show that --seeds prints every seed's number and names which one failed.

Block C · trap two

The requirement itself can be wrong

So far we have assumed the specification is correct and asked whether the controller meets it. Now invert the question.

Requirements review. Checking that a requirement is verifiable at all — before anyone writes a test for it. In DO-178C this happens before verification, not after.
A requirement that no implementation could ever satisfy is a defect. It is a defect in the requirement, not in your controller.
This is the slide that separates a student who follows a spec from an engineer who owns one.

Block C · a real defect

Mission M2 asks the impossible

REQ-M2-1 demands tracking RMSE below 0.15 m on a fast figure-eight.

But the loop closes on the estimate. So measure the estimator itself on that same trajectory:

M2 tracking RMSEvs the 0.15 m bar
Estimation noise floor0.147 mthe hard physical limit
Best tuned LinearMPC0.258 m1.7× over
Stock LinearMPC0.364 m2.4× over
CascadePID baseline0.464 m3.1× over
A controller that tracked its own estimate perfectly would still miss the truth by ≈0.147 m. The requirement asks you to fly more accurately than you can perceive.
Emphasise the first row is a property of the sensors, not of any controller. No tuning reaches it.

Block C · the other direction

A requirement can also be too loose

An unreachable bar is one failure mode. The opposite is quieter and more dangerous.

Too tight
Nothing can pass. Obvious quickly — everybody fails, somebody complains, it gets found.
Too loose
Everything passes, including things that should not. Nobody complains. It is found by an accident.
In your own spec. REQ-M3-2 (transient < 0.50 m) is passed by every controller we have tried, including the deliberately-broken one. It is carrying almost no weight. That is worth knowing about your own evidence: a requirement everything passes is not protecting you from anything.
Genuinely true of REQ-M3-2 in the current spec. Honest, and it sets up viva question B8.

Block C · the honest footnote

How this was found

This defect was in the course specification until 2026-08-25, when a seed flag was added to the harness and the floor was measured for the first time.

  • The bars were set so the classical baseline would fail them — and it does.
  • They were never checked against what any controller could achieve on estimated state.
  • M1 and M2 were therefore unreachable by construction, and nobody had noticed.
It is documented rather than quietly deleted, because the mistake teaches more than its absence. M1 and M2 stay in the harness as your worked example.
Modelling intellectual honesty. Students remember the instructor admitting a spec bug far longer than the bug.

Lab · what good looks like

Your Week-11 deliverable

Integrity

How the certification run actually happens

  • certify.py --student <your ID> --mission M3 is run once, by me, from a fresh clone of your repo at tag final, with the network off.
  • Same command shape, same seed, same procedure for every student — with your ID, so it is your aircraft.
  • Put your student ID in your README. A run with the wrong ID certifies an aircraft that is not yours and does not count.
  • “It worked on my machine” scores nothing. The submitted code is what gets tested.
  • Run it yourself first, so my run is a confirmation and not a surprise.
If your report's numbers and my run disagree, that is an integrity matter, not a bug report.
State this plainly and early. It is much kinder now than in Week 15.

Before you go

Questions worth asking now

  • “What if my baseline already passes M3?” — Tell me. We will look at it together; it usually means a leak.
  • “Can I change my Week-9 controller before freezing it?” — Yes, until Week 12. After the freeze, only with sign-off.
  • “Can I work with a classmate?” — Discuss the theory freely, read each other's code. But you each certify your own vehicle, and copied tuning does not transfer.
  • “Why are my numbers not 0.540 m?” — Because 0.540 m is the default vehicle's. Yours is a different aircraft.
  • “What if MPC does not help?” — Then you write that up rigorously and it can still score well. See the fallback rule.
  • “Do I need do-mpc or CasADi?” — No. Everything works in quadsim with NumPy.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  • A demonstration selects for luck. A certification is built so it cannot.
  • Requirement → verification method → evidence, with traceability between them. Nothing ships on “it worked when I ran it.”
  • A requirement that cannot be verified is a defect in the requirement — M2 is your worked example.
  • One PASS is not evidence. The seed is part of the experiment; report the spread.
  • The reference baseline fails REQ-M3-1 at 0.540 m on the default vehicle; yours fails at your own number, on your own aircraft. Next week: the method that closes it.
