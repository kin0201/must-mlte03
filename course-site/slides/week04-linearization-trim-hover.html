Formally, once

Trim: a definition, and how many there are

Definition (trim / equilibrium). A pair \( (\mathbf x_0, \mathbf u_0) \) with \( f(\mathbf x_0, \mathbf u_0) = \mathbf 0 \): held there exactly, with no disturbance, the aircraft stays there forever.
  • We solved this last week: \( \phi = \theta = 0 \), \( \boldsymbol\omega = \mathbf 0 \), \( T = mg \), with \( (x,y,z,\psi) \) free.
  • So hover is a 4-parameter family of trim points — and because the dynamics do not depend on \( (x,y,z,\psi) \), they all share one \( (A,B) \).
  • That invariance is a gift: design once, use anywhere in the room.
Contrast with a fixed-wing aircraft. There, trim depends on airspeed, altitude and mass — so autopilots carry a schedule of \( (A,B) \) pairs and interpolate. The quadrotor's hover invariance is why one controller flies the whole flight envelope we care about, and why this course can be honest with a single linearisation.
The 'one (A,B) for the whole family' point is worth stressing; it is why the rest of the course is tractable.

Derivation · click to advance interactive

Taylor, in the only form we need

1write the state as trim plus deviation: \( \mathbf x = \mathbf x_0 + \delta\mathbf x \), \( \mathbf u = \mathbf u_0 + \delta\mathbf u \) Change of variables. From now on every symbol means 'how far from hover', not 'where in the room'. Forgetting this is the commonest Lab-4 bug.
2\( f(\mathbf x_0+\delta\mathbf x, \mathbf u_0+\delta\mathbf u) = \underbrace{f(\mathbf x_0,\mathbf u_0)}_{=\,\mathbf 0} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}\delta\mathbf x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf u}\delta\mathbf u + O(\delta^2) \) Taylor about the trim. The constant term vanishes because it is an equilibrium — that is the whole reason we bothered to find one.
3\( \dot{\delta\mathbf x} = A\,\delta\mathbf x + B\,\delta\mathbf u \) Drop the quadratic remainder and rename the Jacobians. A linear time-invariant system — the single most-studied object in engineering.
4valid while \( \lVert O(\delta^2)\rVert \ll \lVert A\delta\mathbf x\rVert \) The fine print, made quantitative. For our trigonometric terms that means roughly \( |\phi|,|\theta| \lesssim 20^\circ \) — the number behind the demo tilt limit.

Everything from Week 5 to Week 12 is designed on this equation. Week 13 is the first method that does not need it.

The equilibrium

What is hover trim?

  • Level — zero roll, pitch, yaw: \( \boldsymbol\eta=\mathbf 0 \).
  • At rest — zero linear velocity and body rates: \( \mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf 0 \).
  • Thrust balances weight — \( T=mg \), pointing \(+\)body-z (which, level, is world up).
  • Zero torque — \( \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf 0 \).
Convention check (z-up / ENU): gravity is \( (0,0,-g) \), thrust acts along \(+\)body-z, so \[ \mathbf u_{eq}=\begin{bmatrix} mg \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\qquad f(\mathbf x_{eq},\mathbf u_{eq})=\mathbf 0. \]

In quadsim this is exactly analysis.hover_equilibrium(p) → returns (x_eq, u_eq) with u_eq = [p.weight, 0, 0, 0].

Stress: equilibrium means the state-derivative vanishes. Not that nothing is happening physically — the motors are spinning hard to make thrust = weight — but the *state* is stationary.

The design model

Linearize by finite differences

Write the state as a small deviation from trim, \( \mathbf x=\mathbf x_{eq}+\delta\mathbf x,\ \mathbf u=\mathbf u_{eq}+\delta\mathbf u \). To first order:

\[ \dot{\delta\mathbf x}\approx A\,\delta\mathbf x + B\,\delta\mathbf u,\qquad A=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}\right|_{eq},\quad B=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf u}\right|_{eq} \]
  • We never differentiate by hand — we hit the same nonlinear \(f\) with central differences.
  • \(A\) is 12×12 (state→state), \(B\) is 12×4 (wrench→state).
  • Column \(i\) of \(A\): \( \dfrac{f(\mathbf x_{eq}+\varepsilon\mathbf e_i,\mathbf u_{eq})-f(\mathbf x_{eq}-\varepsilon\mathbf e_i,\mathbf u_{eq})}{2\varepsilon} \).
Built from the model the students fly — not a separate black box. Same \(f\) = dynamics.state_derivative, so the linear model can never silently drift from the sim.
Central differences (vs forward) so the truncation error is O(eps^2). eps=1e-6 by default. Because f is built from the real dynamics, A and B inherit the conventions automatically — no NED/ENU sign bug.

Formally, once

Linearisation is a promise with a radius

Definition. About an equilibrium \( (\mathbf x_0, \mathbf u_0) \) with \( f(\mathbf x_0,\mathbf u_0)=\mathbf 0 \): \[ \dot{\delta\mathbf x} \approx A\,\delta\mathbf x + B\,\delta\mathbf u, \qquad A = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}\Big|_{0}, \quad B = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf u}\Big|_{0} \] valid while the quadratic remainder stays small against the linear term. Everything from Week 5 to Week 12 designs on \( (A,B) \) — so the radius of that promise is a number worth knowing.
Worked example (how far does "small angle" stretch?). The worst nonlinearity near hover is trigonometric. Relative error of \( \sin\theta \approx \theta \): \( 5^\circ \to 0.13\% \quad\cdot\quad 15^\circ \to 1.1\% \quad\cdot\quad 30^\circ \to 4.7\% \quad\cdot\quad 45^\circ \to 10.3\% \). Our tilt limit (12–25°) keeps the design model honest to ~1–3%. A racing turn at 60° does not — which is precisely why the M2 example in Week 11 cannot be certified by anything hover-linearised, and why Wk-12 mentions nonlinear MPC as an escape hatch.
Remark (numerical Jacobians, done properly). Central differences \( \frac{f(x+\epsilon)-f(x-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon} \) with \( \epsilon \approx 10^{-5} \)–\(10^{-6}\) on unit-scaled states; verify entries you can derive by hand. Too-small \( \epsilon \) drowns in float noise (error \( \sim \delta/\epsilon \)), too-large leaves the linear regime (error \( \sim \epsilon^2 \)) — the sweet spot minimises their sum.
The percent ladder is the quotable artifact. Master-level nuance: A's accuracy matters less once feedback is on (feedback forgives model error) — but the POLES' qualitative story must be right.

Read the matrices, entry by entry

\( (A,B) \) at hover is mostly zeros — and every non-zero tells a story

The five stories in \(A\).
  • \( \partial \dot{\mathbf p}/\partial \mathbf v = I_3 \) — position integrates velocity.
  • \( \partial \dot{\boldsymbol\eta}/\partial \boldsymbol\omega = I_3 \) — \( W(0)=I \): angles integrate rates at hover.
  • \( \partial \ddot{x}/\partial \theta = +g, \quad \partial \ddot{y}/\partial \phi = -g \) — the tilt-to-translate coupling, the only bridge between the halves.
  • Everything else: 0. No spring pulls position back; no damper resists tilt.
The two stories in \(B\).
  • \( \partial \ddot z/\partial T = 1/m = 1.54 \) — thrust climbs.
  • \( \partial \dot\omega_i/\partial \tau_i = 1/I_{ii} \approx 435 \) (roll/pitch) — tiny torques, huge angular accelerations: this is why attitude is the fast loop.

Block-triangular structure ⇒ at hover the system decouples into four chains: (x,θ), (y,φ), z, ψ — the licence for per-axis design in Wks 5–7.

(3 min) — your Lab-3 code printed these matrices. Circle the g entries and the 1/I entries on your own printout; write one sentence on what a wind gust (a force, not a torque) touches first.
"Read matrices like prose" is a transferable master skill. The 435 number invariably surprises — connect to why Kp=180 on attitude is sane while Kp=180 on position would be absurd.

Read the structure

\( (A,B) \) is mostly zeros — the sparsity is the physics

Every filled cell is a physical statement; every empty cell is an absent mechanism. The two amber cells are the only bridge from attitude to position.

  • Teal cells (value 1): pure integrators — position integrates velocity, angles integrate rates.
  • Amber cells (\( \pm g \)): the tilt-to-translate coupling. Delete them and the aircraft can climb but never move sideways.
  • Everything else is zero: no spring returns position, no damper resists rotation. Nothing pulls the aircraft back to anything.
Why the emptiness matters. A system with no restoring terms is not merely 'hard to fly' — it is incapable of flying itself. Feedback is not an improvement to this aircraft; it is a load-bearing component, as much as the arms.
Let them stare at the sparsity map. The emptiness is the most persuasive argument for control that exists in the course.

Poles, eigenvalues, and what 'stability' actually asserts

Definition. For \( \dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x \), solutions are combinations of \( e^{\lambda_i t} \) where \( \lambda_i \) are the eigenvalues of \( A \) (the poles). Then: \( \mathrm{Re}\,\lambda < 0 \) for all \( i \) ⇒ asymptotically stable; any \( \mathrm{Re}\,\lambda > 0 \) ⇒ unstable; \( \mathrm{Re}\,\lambda = 0 \) ⇒ marginal, and the linear model declines to answer.
Pole locationBehaviourOur quad
\( \lambda < 0 \), realdecaysnone
\( \lambda = 0 \)integrates forevermost of them
\( \lambda = 0 \) repeateddouble integrator: drifts and accelerates awayposition–tilt chains
complex, \( \mathrm{Re} < 0 \)damped oscillationwhat we will create
What a double integrator does. \( \ddot y = -g\phi \) with a constant \( \phi = 1^\circ \): after 3 s the aircraft has moved \( \tfrac12 (0.17)(9) \approx 0.77 \) m — and it is still accelerating.

A one-degree trim error crosses the room in five seconds. That is what 'marginally stable' feels like in a classroom.
The one-degree/five-seconds calculation is the most visceral version of this argument; use it every year.

Formally, once

Controllability — can we get anywhere from here?

Definition (Kalman, 1960). \( (A,B) \) is controllable if for any initial and any target state there exists an input that gets you there in finite time. Test: \( \mathcal C = [\,B \;\; AB \;\; A^2B \;\; \dots \;\; A^{n-1}B\,] \) has full rank \( n = 12 \).
  • Our hover model: rank 12 ✓ — with four inputs and twelve states, and no wasted directions.
  • The chain \( B, AB, A^2B \) is the algebra noticing what you already know physically: torque → rate → angle → lateral acceleration → velocity → position. Four matrix products for four integrations.
  • This rank test is the entry ticket for LQR (Week 7) and for the pole placement that makes the cascade work at all.
What failure would look like. If a rotor died, \( B \) loses a column and yaw controllability degrades — the algebra would tell you which axis you can no longer command, before any flight test. That is the practical value of an abstract rank condition.
Frame controllability as 'the algebra confirming the physics', then note it becomes a precondition in Wk 7.

Why we need feedback

The open-loop poles sit at the origin

The poles are the eigenvalues of \(A\). For hover, many of them are exactly zero:

\[ \lambda_i=\operatorname{eig}(A),\qquad \text{several } \lambda_i = 0 \;\;(\text{Re}=0). \]
  • Position integrates velocity; attitude integrates rate ⇒ chains of integrators.
  • Poles on the imaginary axis ⇒ marginally stable, NOT asymptotically stable.
  • A torque bias is never corrected — it drifts away. This is why the open-loop quad tips.
Marginal stability is the whole motivation for the course: feedback must pull these poles into the left half-plane. Wk 5 does it for attitude; Wk 7 (LQR) & Wk 11–12 (MPC) for the rest.
Don't say "unstable" loosely — at hover the linearization gives poles AT the origin (integrators), i.e. marginally stable. The nonlinear quad then diverges because nothing returns it. Either way: feedback required.

The pole picture

Where the poles live — and where feedback must move them

Open loop: integrator chains parked on the imaginary axis. Closed loop (Wks 5–7): feedback drags them left; σ sets decay, the pair's angle sets damping ζ.

Can we even fix it?

Controllability: full rank 12

Before designing any controller, check the four inputs can actually steer all twelve states. Build the controllability matrix and take its rank:

\[ \mathcal C=\big[\,B\ \ AB\ \ A^2B\ \ \cdots\ \ A^{11}B\,\big],\qquad \operatorname{rank}\mathcal C = 12. \]
Full rank (\(=n=12\)) ⇒ every state is reachable from the wrench \( [T,\tau_x,\tau_y,\tau_z] \). This is the green light for LQR (Wk 7): a stabilizing gain exists. In quadsim:
C, rank = analysis.controllability(A, B) # rank == 12
Controllability does not depend on units being nice; rank tol is 1e-6 in the code. Tie underactuation (Wk5) to controllability: only 4 inputs, yet the coupling through tilt makes all 12 states reachable — that is why a quad can fly at all.

Formally, once

Modes and eigenvectors: what the poles are doing to what

  • An eigenvalue answers how fast; its eigenvector answers which states move together.
  • For the lateral block, the repeated zero eigenvalue has eigenvectors mixing \( \phi \) and \( v_y \) — the algebra saying 'tilt and drift are the same motion'.
  • You cannot fix drift without touching tilt. Any controller that tries is fighting the eigenstructure.
Making it concrete. Start with \( \phi = 2^\circ \), everything else zero, no control. The linear model predicts \( \phi \) stays at 2°, \( v_y \) grows linearly, \( y \) grows quadratically.

Run the nonlinear sim for 5 s and compare: the same, to within a few percent. A confirmation that the linearisation is telling the truth in this regime — and a check worth doing before trusting any design built on it.
Eigenvectors are usually skipped; the 'tilt and drift are one motion' reading makes them worth five minutes.

Verification, not faith

Does the linear model actually predict the nonlinear one?

  1. Pick a deviation: \( \theta_0 = 5^\circ \), all else at trim.
  2. Simulate the nonlinear \( f \) for 2 s.
  3. Simulate \( \dot{\delta\mathbf x} = A\delta\mathbf x \) from the same start.
  4. Overlay \( x(t) \). Repeat at \( 15^\circ \), \( 30^\circ \), \( 45^\circ \).
What you will see. 5°: curves indistinguishable.
15°: ~1% apart at 2 s.
30°: ~5%, visibly diverging.
45°: ~12%, different stories.

The numbers match the \( \sin\theta \) error ladder from Week 1 — as they must, since that is the dominant nonlinearity.
The habit being taught. Never inherit a model on trust. Two simulations and one overlay tell you the radius of validity of everything you are about to design. It costs ten minutes and it is the difference between engineering and hoping.

Why RK4, and where the 4 comes from

1we need \( \mathbf x(t+h) \) from \( \mathbf x(t) \) and \( f \) The whole problem of simulation: we know the slope everywhere, we want the trajectory.
2Euler: \( \mathbf x + h f(\mathbf x) \) — use the slope at the start for the whole step Simplest possible answer, and it systematically misses whenever the slope changes during the step. Error per step \( O(h^2) \).
3sample the slope four times: start, twice at the midpoint, end The key idea. Instead of trusting one slope, probe how the slope evolves across the step — a poor man's higher derivative, obtained without ever differentiating \( f \).
4\( \mathbf x_{n+1} = \mathbf x_n + \tfrac{h}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4) \) Weighted average, midpoints counted double. The weights are chosen to cancel error terms up to \( h^4 \) — hence 'fourth order': halve the step and the error falls 16×.
5cost: 4 evaluations of \( f \) per step, versus 1 for Euler The trade. Four times the work per step buys you far more than four times the accuracy — which is why nearly every physics simulator you will ever use is RK4 or a cousin.
Students use RK4 constantly and rarely know why it works. Step 3 is the insight; the weights are bookkeeping.

The deciding argument. A discrete controller is a fixed-rate object: it samples, computes, holds. Simulating it with an adaptive integrator means your controller runs at a rate that does not exist on any real machine — and gains tuned there are gains tuned for an imaginary aircraft.
The one thing to check. Fixed step demands you verify the step is small enough. Halve \( \Delta t \) to 2.5 ms and re-run: if the trajectory changes visibly, 5 ms was too coarse. Ours does not — a two-minute check that licenses everything after it.
The 'halve dt and compare' convergence check is the standard professional habit; make it a lab checkpoint.

Detail, done properly

The simulation loop, and where the controller sits in it

for k in range(n_steps):
    t = k * dt

    # ---- 1. sense: what the controller is allowed to know ----
    meas = sensors.read(x, t)        # Wk 8 replaces this with noise + rates

    # ---- 2. think: your code, once per step, fixed budget ----
    u = controller.control(t, meas)  # returns [T, tau_x, tau_y, tau_z]

    # ---- 3. allocate + saturate: physics has the last word ----
    f = np.clip(alloc @ u, 0.0, p.f_max)
    u_actual = mixer @ f             # what the aircraft ACTUALLY gets

    # ---- 4. integrate the plant ----
    x = rk4_step(x, u_actual, dt)

    log.append(t, x, u, u_actual, meas)
  • Step 3 is the honest one: we log both what you asked for and what the aircraft delivered. The gap between them is a diagnostic you will use all term.
  • The controller is called exactly once per step — the same discipline as firmware.
Why the structure matters. This loop is a miniature of the real flight-control task: sense, think, actuate, repeat, under a fixed budget. Weeks 8, 11 and 13 change what goes in the 'think' box and leave the rest untouched.
Point at each numbered block and name the week that will modify it. The u vs u_actual logging habit pays off in every later debugging session.

Worked example · predict, then check

Two seconds of open-loop flight, by hand

Start at hover. At \( t=0 \), hold \( \tau_y = 0.01 \) N·m for 2 s with \( T = mg \). Predict \( \theta(2) \) and \( x(2) \) using the linear model.

  1. \( \dot q = \tau_y/I_{yy} = 0.01/2.3\times10^{-3} = 4.35 \) rad/s².
  2. \( \theta(t) = \tfrac12 (4.35) t^2 \Rightarrow \theta(2) = 8.7 \) rad — impossible, that is 500°.
  3. Something is wrong with the question, not the arithmetic: the aircraft leaves the linear regime within about 0.3 s.
  4. Redo for \( t = 0.3 \) s: \( \theta = 0.196 \) rad \( = 11^\circ \), \( x = \tfrac{1}{24} g (4.35) (0.3)^4 \approx 0.014 \) m.
The lesson is the failure. A tiny 0.01 N·m torque — one sixtieth of available authority — flips the aircraft past vertical in under a second if nobody intervenes.

That is what 'no passive stability' means numerically. It also shows the linear model policing its own boundary: when it predicts 500°, it is telling you the question left its jurisdiction.
(2 min) — How long until \( \theta \) reaches 30° (the edge of good linearity)? (\( t = \sqrt{2(0.52)/4.35} \approx 0.49 \) s. Your controller has half a second to notice and respond — which sets the bandwidth requirement for Week 5.)
The 0.49 s answer is a genuine specification derivation: it tells them why the attitude loop must run at tens of Hz.

Concept check · 00:50

Predict before you compute

Q1 — The linearized hover model has eigenvalues at the origin (integrator chains), none in the right half-plane. So why did we keep calling the quad "unstable"?

Marginal + double-integrator chains = practically unstable. Any disturbance integrates twice into runaway position/attitude drift; and the nonlinear coupling feeds errors between axes. "No exponential blow-up in the linear model" is cold comfort — watch the Wk-1 tip-over again. Feedback must place those poles left of the axis (Wk 5–7).

Q2 — Your finite-difference B matrix has a column of zeros. First suspect?

Perturbation size / units on that input. Too-small ε drowns in float noise, too-large leaves the linear regime; or you perturbed a motor command where the model expects a wrench. Numerical Jacobians are a craft: always sanity-check entries you can derive by hand (∂a_z/∂T = 1/m = 1.54).

One level deeper · continuous law, discrete computer

Choosing \( \Delta t \): rules with numbers, not folklore

Definition (zero-order-hold discretisation). Holding \( \mathbf u \) constant over a step, the exact discrete model is \( A_d = e^{A\Delta t}, \; B_d = \int_0^{\Delta t} e^{A s} ds\, B \); for small \( A\Delta t \), \( A_d \approx I + A\Delta t \), \( B_d \approx B\Delta t \). MPC (Wk 11) uses exactly this \( (A_d, B_d) \).
Worked example (is 200 Hz enough?). Fastest loop we will close: attitude at \( \omega_n \approx 13 \) rad/s (\( \approx 2.1 \) Hz). Rules of thumb: sample \( \ge 20\times \) the closed-loop bandwidth ⇒ ≥ 42 Hz for control; RK4 stability for oscillatory modes wants \( \omega_n \Delta t \lesssim 0.5 \) ⇒ \( \Delta t \lesssim 38 \) ms. Our 5 ms is ~10× inside both — comfortable, and it leaves room for the motor lag (\( \tau_m \approx 40 \) ms) to stay the binding constraint, not the mathematics.
Remark (the firmware's clock is yours to verify). quadsim's \( \Delta t \) is exact by construction. On the STM32, the loop period is whatever your code achieves under load — measure it with a toggling GPIO pin or timestamps, put the number on the wall (checklist §2). Gains tuned at one rate are silently wrong at another unless rate-normalised.
Two takeaways: 200 Hz is generous by design, and "measure, don't assume" transfers to hardware. The ω_nΔt ≤ 0.5 rule comes straight from the widget they just used.

import numpy as np

p = QuadParams()

# 1) Linearize the real nonlinear model about hover

Formally, once

Observability: the mirror image of controllability

Definition. \( (A,C) \) is observable if the state can be reconstructed from a finite record of the outputs \( \mathbf y = C\mathbf x \). Test: \( \mathcal O = [\,C;\, CA;\, \dots;\, CA^{n-1}\,] \) has rank \( n \).
  • Controllability asks can I steer it?; observability asks can I see it?. Same algebra, transposed.
  • It decides whether Week 8's estimator is even possible — no filter can recover a state the sensors cannot distinguish.
  • With a full IMU plus a position fix, our quadrotor is observable. Remove the position fix and \( (x,y) \) become unobservable: the aircraft knows how it is pointing, never where it is.
A real consequence. That last line is exactly why indoor flight without optical flow is impossible to stabilise in position — and why the flow deck is not an accessory but a structural requirement for L3 in the Week-10 demonstration.

The rank test predicts the flight failure before anyone builds the aircraft.
Pairing observability with the flow-deck requirement makes an abstract rank test operationally meaningful.

A second view of the same system

Transfer functions — one line, for the students who have met them

Laplace the lateral chain and the four integrators become four poles at the origin:

\[ \frac{Y(s)}{T_x(s)} = \frac{-g}{I_{xx}\, s^4} \]
  • Four poles at \( s=0 \) ⇒ \( -360^\circ \) of phase at every frequency.
  • Classical stability needs phase margin at crossover; with \( 1/s^4 \) there is none to spare — so a single loop cannot work, no matter the gain.
  • Add an inner attitude loop and the outer loop sees \( 1/s^2 \) instead: \( -180^\circ \), which a PD can rescue.
Why the course uses state space anyway. Transfer functions are elegant for one input and one output. We have four and twelve, coupled. State space handles that natively — and it is the only language in which LQR, Kalman and MPC can even be stated.

Take the insight (\( 1/s^4 \) is unmanageable; cascading fixes it) and leave the machinery.
One slide of frequency-domain contact for students from a classical control background, without derailing the state-space narrative.

Concept check

Three questions before the lab

Q1 — You linearise about a tilted trim (steady 20° forward flight). Does \( A \) stay block-diagonal?

No — the blocks couple. Tilting mixes the axes, so longitudinal and vertical motion interact. This is precisely why fixed-wing autopilots gain-schedule and why our hover-only design is such a simplification.

Q2 — Your numerical \( B \) has \( \partial\dot v_z / \partial T = 1.54 \). Sanity-check it.

\( 1/m = 1/0.65 = 1.538 \) ✓ Every entry you can predict by hand should be predicted by hand. Numerical Jacobians are code, and code has bugs; hand-checkable entries are your assertions.

Q3 — A classmate says "the poles are at zero, so the aircraft is neither stable nor unstable — it just sits there." Correct them.

Marginal in the linear model means the model declines to answer; the nonlinear aircraft diverges. A double integrator turns any bias into quadratic drift — one degree of tilt crosses the room in five seconds. "Sits there" is what it does for the first tenth of a second only.

Q3 is the conceptual crux of the week and a favourite exam question; insist on the double-integrator argument, not intuition.

Detail, done properly

Numerical Jacobians: the bias-noise trade, quantified

Central difference, one column at a time:

\[ \big[A\big]_{:,j} \approx \frac{f(\mathbf x_0 + \epsilon\mathbf e_j,\mathbf u_0) - f(\mathbf x_0 - \epsilon\mathbf e_j,\mathbf u_0)}{2\epsilon} \]
  • Truncation error \( \sim \epsilon^2 \) — wants \( \epsilon \) small.
  • Round-off error \( \sim \delta_{machine}/\epsilon \) — wants \( \epsilon \) large.
  • Optimum near \( \epsilon \approx \delta^{1/3} \approx 6\times10^{-6} \) for doubles.
See both failures. \( \epsilon = 10^{-2} \): the tilt is no longer small — you measure a chord, not a tangent. Error ~0.3%.
\( \epsilon = 10^{-12} \): the two evaluations agree to 12 digits; their difference is pure noise. Error ~100%.
\( \epsilon = 10^{-6} \): error \( < 10^{-8} \). ✓

Scale each state first — 1 metre and 1 radian are not comparable perturbations.

Same trade-off, different clothes: too little smoothing is noise, too much is bias. You meet it again in Week 8's filter and Week 13's learning rate.

The 'same trade in three places' framing is a genuine unifying thread — name it each time it appears.

Detail, done properly

Logging: designing the data you will need at 2 a.m.

  • Log time, full state, commanded \( \mathbf u \), delivered \( \mathbf u \), per-motor \( f_i \), and every controller internal (each PID term separately).
  • Logging only the output is the commonest self-inflicted wound: when it misbehaves you cannot tell which term misbehaved.
  • Fixed-rate arrays, not lists of dicts. You will replay these thousands of times.
What good logging answers instantly. • Did the mixer clamp? (commanded vs delivered)
• Is the integrator winding up? (its own channel)
• Is D amplifying noise? (D-term vs gyro trace)
• Was the loop late? (timestamp deltas)

Each unanswered question costs a re-run — and on hardware a re-run costs a battery and a flight.
Lab 1 requirement: your harness must answer all four questions above without re-running. That is the graded deliverable, not the plot.
Framing logging as a spec with checkable criteria is the highest-leverage habit for the Week-10 diagnosis and the final-project evidence kit.

The instructor's aircraft

The vendor linearised it exactly as we did

WHEELTEC's own development manual reaches this model — identical to the one you will derive today:

\[ \ddot x = g\theta, \quad \ddot y = -g\phi, \quad \ddot z = u_1/m \] \[ \ddot\phi = u_2/I_{xx}, \quad \ddot\theta = u_3/I_{yy}, \quad \ddot\psi = u_4/I_{zz} \]

with \( u_1 = \sum f_i - mg \) — note the \( -mg \): they define the input as the deviation from hover thrust, exactly the \( \delta \)-coordinates this lecture insists on.

Their parameters, measured. \( m = 0.079 + 0.060 + 4(0.014) = 0.195 \) kg — frame, battery, four motors, counted individually.

\( I_{xx} = \tfrac{1}{12}m_1(0.069^2 + 0.012^2) + 4m_2(0.0636)^2 \)
\( I_{zz} = \tfrac{1}{12}m_1(0.069^2 + 0.069^2) + 4m_2(0.0900)^2 \)

A slab plus four point masses — precisely the estimate you did by hand in Week 2, used in a shipping product.
Showing that a commercial vendor uses the same hand-derivation the students just did is enormously validating. Point at the -mg and connect to delta coordinates.

The aircraft in this room

Six two-state subsystems — the cascade, written in state space

SubsystemStateInput\( B \) entry
x-translation\( (x, \dot x) \)\( \theta \) — an angle!\( g \)

Look at the first two rows. The x-subsystem's input is an attitude angle, and that angle is the output of the roll/pitch subsystem.

That is the cascade — not as an architectural preference, but as an algebraic fact about the linearised model. Six tiny 2×2 problems, chained. The vendor solves six separate 2-state LQRs and stacks them; we build two nested PD loops. Same decomposition, two spellings.

This is worth sitting with: two independent teams, one Chinese commercial vendor and one teaching simulator, arrived at the same partition of the problem — because the partition is in the physics, not in the taste.

This slide is the strongest possible evidence that the Wk-4 decomposition is real. It also pre-explains the Wk-7 per-axis LQR design.

Before you leave

The traps, named

The trapThe truth
"Poles at the origin, none in the RHP — so hover is stable."Marginally stable: double-integrator chains turn any bias into unbounded drift. "Not exploding in the linear model" is not "flyable".
"My controller oscillates — the gains are wrong."First ask: physics or numerics? A too-coarse integrator manufactures oscillations (you just watched it). quadsim's 200 Hz RK4 acquits numerics; on hardware, an unmeasured loop rate does not.
"A linear model is 'wrong', so why bother."It is locally excellent (1% at 15°) and buys the entire design toolbox: poles, LQR, Kalman, linear MPC. The professional move is knowing the radius, not refusing the promise.
"Finite differences: smaller \( \epsilon \) is always better."Below ~\(10^{-7}\), float cancellation dominates and the Jacobian gets worse. Numerical differentiation is a bias–noise trade, like everything else this term.
Row 2 is the debugging habit that saves the most lab hours. Row 3 frames the course's philosophy of models.
