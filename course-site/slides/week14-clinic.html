The mission, restated

M3 — payload drop. One mission, every student, a different aircraft.

  • The quadrotor holds position at [0, 0, 1.5].
  • At t = 5 s the mass steps down: a package is released. (0.65 → 0.45 kg on the course-default vehicle; your two masses come from your student ID.)
  • The controller is never told. There is no flag, no event, no second instance — one continuous controller object, running through the step.
  • It must notice through feedback and correct. That is the whole engineering problem.
  • Total run: 15 s. Graded on estimator seed 0, on your own vehicle.
Why the controller is not told. Because on a real aircraft nothing tells it. A release mechanism fires, thrust is suddenly far too high for the new mass (about 44% on the course-default vehicle; your own figure depends on your drop fraction), and the only evidence available to the autopilot is that the vehicle is climbing when it asked to hover.

A controller that must be informed of a plant change is not a controller; it is a schedule. M3 exists to make that distinction concrete.

One command produces the verdict — and it must carry your student ID:

python certify.py --controller <yours> --student <your ID> --mission M3
python -m quadsim.student_params <your ID>    # your own mass, arm, k_torque, inertia

Every student certifies against a different quadcopter, derived deterministically from the student ID — same ID, same aircraft, always. A run with the wrong ID is a run on somebody else's aircraft and does not count, so record your ID in your README.

Restate rather than re-teach — they have seen this three times. The two sentences that matter today: the controller is never told, and the run must carry their own student ID. Have them run student_params live and read their pre-drop mass aloud; the ones who cannot are your first bench visits.

The specification

Three requirements — that is the entire spec

Certified. All three requirements PASS. Two out of three is NOT CERTIFIED — there is no partial credit inside the tool, and there is no negotiation about it afterwards.
Read the three carefully — they are not the same test. REQ-M3-2 asks how badly did it lurch. REQ-M3-3 asks did it settle. REQ-M3-1 is the envelope over everything after the drop.

A controller can settle beautifully and still fail REQ-M3-1 on a single spike it recovered from. That is exactly what happens to the baseline.

Checkpoint

Reading a certification report properly

  • A verdict line is worthless without its measured value. "PASS" tells you nothing about margin; max = 0.473 m does.
  • Report the value and the bar together, every time: 0.473 m against 0.50 m.
  • Two controllers can both be CERTIFIED and be nothing alike — one at 0.20 m, one at 0.49 m.
The sentence I want to hear at the viva. "I pass REQ-M3-1 at ___ m against the 0.50 m bar — a ___% margin, which is thin, and here is the seed sweep that shows how thin."

Not "I passed". Everyone passed, or did not; the mark is in what you know about how.
(2 min) — Your current run: name REQ-M3-1's measured value and its margin to the bar, out loud, without looking it up. (If you cannot, that is today's first task — not a hypothetical one.)
Run the two-minute turn for real: pick three students at random and have them say their REQ-M3-1 value and margin without looking. Whoever cannot has found their first task. The sentence in the example box is exactly what you will listen for in the Week-15 viva — say so.

Honesty requirement

Graded on seed 0 — but you must say what the other seeds do

  • The grade uses seed 0, on your vehicle. The seed is fixed, published and identical for everyone; the aircraft is yours.
  • Your report must carry one sentence on the spread from --seeds 0,1,2. One sentence — not a section.
  • Across 8 seeds the same CascadePID ranges 0.211 m – 0.721 m on REQ-M3-1 (course-default vehicle).

The dry run

Do it exactly as the real thing, or it proves nothing

The dry run. Reproduce the marking environment on your own machine, deliberately and pessimistically: fresh clone · tag final · network off · certify.py on both the frozen baseline and the current submission.

Every one of those four conditions exists because a previous cohort lost marks to its absence.

The test is not "does it work". It is "does it work for someone who is not you, on a machine that is not yours, from what you actually pushed".

Those are different questions, and only the second one gets marked. The gap between them is the single commonest way a working project scores badly.

Nothing about the dry run is about control theory. It is about the difference between a system that exists and a system that has been delivered — and that distinction is a professional one you will use for the rest of your career.

Four conditions — fresh clone, tag final, network off, both certify runs. Each exists because a previous cohort lost marks to its absence. The line to land: the marked question is not 'does it work' but 'does it work for someone who is not you, on a machine that is not yours, from what you actually pushed'.

The dry run · do this at your bench now

The checklist, literally

  1. Check your student ID — the one in your README, digit by digit.
  2. Push everything, then tag it. git status clean, nothing stashed; git tag final and push the tag. The tag is what gets marked.
  3. Fresh clone into a new empty directory — not your working copy — and check out the tag there.
  4. Network off, physically; build the environment only from what the repo declares.
  5. Run certify.py --controller <baseline> --student <your ID> --mission M3. Save the report.
  6. Run certify.py --controller <yours> --student <your ID> --mission M3. Save the report.
  7. Run --seeds 0,1,2. Record the range.
  8. Read the ID printed in both reports. Same, and yours — or the comparison is void.
  9. Paste both reports into the report file — verbatim, before you forget.
grep -i student README*      # your ID, confirmed
git status                   # must be clean
git tag final && git push --tags

cd /tmp && rm -rf dryrun
git clone <your-repo> dryrun
cd dryrun && git checkout final

Failure mode 1

"It runs on my machine" — the clean-clone test

  • Typical causes: a file never committed · a package installed months ago and never declared · an import that resolves only because your shell exports PYTHONPATH · a notebook that defines a helper your module quietly needs.
  • None of these are visible in your working copy. All of them are fatal at marking time, because the marking run is a clean clone.
The two-minute version. Clone into /tmp, check out final, and run the single certify command. If it produces a report, this failure mode is dead. If it raises, you have found — for free, today — the exact error the marker would have hit.

There is no cheaper mark in the whole project.
(5 min) — Do the clean clone now, before anything else on your list. (Already done it? Do it again after your last commit. The failure mode returns every time you push.)
Five-minute turn: everyone clones into /tmp and runs certify once. Expect two or three ModuleNotFoundError or PYTHONPATH failures in a room of 35 — celebrate them out loud: found for free today, fatal at marking time. Anyone already done it re-does it after their last commit.

Failure mode 2

Ground-truth leakage — the one that voids the result

Leakage. The controller consumes information the real system would not give it: the true mass after the drop, the true state instead of the estimate, or the drop time t = 5 s as a literal. Reading p_light, or anything derived from it, is the same offence.

M3's entire difficulty is that the controller is not told. A controller that is told has solved a different, easier problem — and the certification report will not say so, which is why you must.

It is almost never deliberate. It arrives through convenience: a debug line that read the true state and was never removed; a mass parameter passed in during development "just to check the maths"; a schedule keyed to t > 5.0 that started as a plotting aid.

Grep your own controller for the true-state handle, for the post-drop mass value, and for the literal 5.0 — right now. Finding it yourself costs nothing; me finding it at the viva costs the claim.
A leaked result is not a low mark, it is a void one — the evidence no longer supports the claim being made. Self-declared and fixed today, it is simply a bug you found.
Have everyone run the three greps while you say this. Expect at least one student to find something.

Failure mode 3

Hard-coded mass, hard-coded paths

  • An absolute path — /Users/…/final/data — is a guaranteed failure on any machine but yours.
  • A mass literal baked into the controller is either a portability bug (your pre-drop mass, hard-coded instead of read from the model) or leakage (your post-drop mass, which nothing tells the controller). Both are bad; one is worse. And this year a hard-coded mass is almost certainly the wrong mass — the course-default 0.65 kg is not your aircraft.
  • A config file that is gitignored is the same failure wearing a disguise.
The honest use of the pre-drop mass. Designing your MPC around your own pre-drop mass — the one student_params prints — is legitimate. That is your design model, known before flight, and you should say so.

Using your post-drop mass anywhere in the controller is not, because nothing tells the controller the drop happened.

State your student ID and which mass model you designed against, in one line of the report. That line pre-empts both questions at once.

The clean-clone dry run catches the paths. Only reading your own code catches the mass.

The subtle point: a hard-coded mass is either a portability bug (pre-drop) or leakage (post-drop), and this year it is almost certainly the wrong mass anyway because 0.65 kg is the course default, not theirs. Legitimate use: designing the MPC around their own pre-drop mass from student_params — but they must say so in one line of the report.

Failure modes 4–6

Three ways to lose marks on work you already did

Not verbatim. A tidy summary table you typed is your claim. The tool's raw output — including [FAIL] REQ-M3-1 max = 0.540 m — is evidence. Paste the block, keep the FAIL lines, do not reformat it.

Deleting a FAIL line from the baseline report removes the whole point of having a baseline.
A pass with no seed sentence. One sentence. Its absence reads as either not knowing the spread exists, or knowing and omitting it. Neither is a good look at a viva where the examiner has the 0.211–0.721 range in front of him.
A figure with no stated claim. A caption that says "altitude versus time" describes the axes, which the reader can already see. A caption that says "the baseline overshoots to 0.540 m at t = 5.4 s while MPC peaks at 0.473 m; the difference is the anticipated thrust reduction visible from t = 5.1 s" makes an argument.

Every figure must assert something a reader could disagree with. If it cannot, it is decoration and it is costing you a page.
Verbatim means including the FAIL line — deleting it from the baseline report deletes the reason the baseline exists. The seed sentence is one sentence, not a section. For figures, the test is 'could a reader disagree with the caption?' If not, it is decoration costing a page of the six.

The report

Three required items. Six pages, maximum.

  1. Both certification reports, pasted verbatim — the frozen baseline and your submission. Raw output, FAIL lines intact.
  2. The MPC formulation, written as engineering — model, horizon, weights, constraints, and why each choice. Not a textbook restatement; your actual numbers, with reasons.
  3. One figure + one paragraph on the mechanism — what MPC does differently at the drop, shown and then argued.

Plus the one seed sentence, and your student ID. That is the whole required content.

Separately: the engineering log — one page, 10 marks. What you tried, what failed, what you decided. Written as you went, not reconstructed tonight.

Why the cap is six pages. Because a marginal result explained clearly in four pages is worth more than the same result buried in twelve. The cap forces you to decide what your argument actually is.

Length is not evidence. Every year the strongest reports are among the shortest, and this is not a coincidence.
All 100 marks are individual. Certification 20 · improvement 15 · report depth 15 · code & reproducibility 10 · viva 20 · engineering log + git trail 10 · quizzes 10. The fallback rule caps the four artifact items (60 marks) at 45/60; viva, log and quizzes are unaffected.
Marked on: evidence completeness (are both reports there, verbatim, with your ID?) · formulation quality (are the choices justified with your numbers?) · mechanism (does the figure support the claim in the paragraph?).
Do not enumerate the marks table aloud; it is on the slide and in the brief. Spend the time on the six-page argument: length is not evidence, and the strongest reports are among the shortest every year. The report · the figure

One figure, doing one job

  • Put baseline and MPC on the same axes, same seed, same window. Two lines, one comparison.
  • Draw the 0.50 m bar. A requirement that is not drawn cannot be seen to be met.
  • Mark t = 5 s. The reader should not have to infer where the drop is.
  • Label the two peaks with your two values, not the lecture example's.

Then check your own numbers support every clause. If one does not, delete that clause.

Optional · only after the required three

Stretch evidence — only if you are already finished

  • Constraint-activation plot — bounds drawn, active regions shaded. Shows the constraints are doing work rather than decorating the formulation.
  • Timing histogram — solve time against your control period. Answers "would this run on the aircraft?".
  • Ablation table — shorter horizon, different weights. Shows you chose your parameters.
Order matters. A stretch item added to an incomplete required three is worth nothing. Finish the gate, then stretch.

If you are reading this before your clean clone has run, this slide is not for you yet.

The ablation is the one that most often changes a viva. A student who can say "at N = 6 I revert to baseline behaviour, so the horizon is doing the work" has evidence for a claim the report could otherwise only assert.
