Known to you. The effect classes above, the mission, the estimator, the seed, your own controller — and the nominal numbers you tuned on. That is exactly what a flight-test engineer knows before the first flight of a new airframe: what kind of thing can be wrong, never how much.
Why the ranges are public. So that your diagnosis can be quantitative: "the take-off sag is consistent with +9 % mass" is a claim the instructor can check against the truth. "It was heavier" is not.

The plant also has a floor. A vehicle heavier than its controller believes sags at take-off; on the real thing it sits on the pad, so the simulated one does too.

Emphasise: ranges public, values private. The instructor holds every team's truth (solutions/reveal_team_plant.py) and uses it only to ask questions.

The black-box plant · interactive

The gap explorer — one effect at a time

The nominal cascade PID flies the same square. Switch one effect on, watch the shape of the track change, then the next. Your job in the workshop is the inverse problem: given your team's shape, which combination made it?

Predict before you compute. Before touching a slider: which single effect makes the track overshoot every corner? Which one makes it land off-centre? Which one sags at take-off? Three guesses on paper, then test.

Open it any time: Digital Lab → gap explorer.

Drive it yourself for two minutes: lag alone (rounded corners, late settle), mass alone (sag), gust alone (offset + wander), latency alone (hunting when gains are raised). Then switch all four on and ask "now which was which?" — that is the workshop.

Reading (a) quantitatively. A first-order lag answers a step to 63 % in one time constant. Measure the time for the applied thrust to close 63 % of the gap after the first big command — that is \( \tau_m \), to within the noise. Compare it with the public range.
Why drag has no plot of its own. Drag is velocity-proportional, so it hides inside (a)'s residual and the corner overshoot. Separating it from lag needs a controlled experiment — the gap explorer with everything else off. That is a legitimate thing to write: "lag and drag are confounded in our data; the explorer suggests lag dominates because…"
Teams that build all four plots in the first 25 minutes have an easy afternoon. Push them to (a) and (c) first — those two are unambiguous.

How to write the analysis that earns the marks

Anatomy of a good gap paragraph

Weak (observation only). "On the team plant the RMSE went from 0.23 m to 0.46 m, probably because of wind and other unmodelled effects. More tuning would help."

No mechanism, no evidence, no test. Scores the floor.

Strong (mechanism + evidence + verdict). "Altitude dipped 0.11 m in the first 0.8 s of take-off and recovered over ≈2 s (Fig. 2). A thrust-mass mismatch produces exactly this: initial acceleration \( g(m/m_{\text{true}}-1) \), then recovery at the z-integrator's rate. The dip size implies ≈ +9 % mass, inside the published range. Doubling the integrator clamp halved the dip (0.05 m) and left the nominal column unchanged."

Names the chain, shows the numbers, closes the loop with an intervention.

Template: symptom (quantified) → mechanism (course concept, named) → evidence (plot) → intervention or bound. One paragraph per gap; three good gaps beat ten vague ones. The instructor can check your estimate against the hidden truth — a wrong number with a right argument still scores; a right number with no argument does not.

Read both aloud; the room hears the difference instantly. This template is also exactly the viva's expected answer-shape — say so.

The sixth gap, which is yours to find. Interactions. Lightning debriefs

The debrief protocol — five minutes, two slides

  1. The gap (1 min): the two-column table, fingerprint on the slide, one picture of the track.
  2. Three mechanisms (2½ min): each one sentence + one plot. Effect → evidence → why the evidence follows.
  3. The one change (1 min): what, why, before/after on both columns.
  4. One question from me (30 s) — always some form of "how do you know?"
A model answer, compressed. 'RMSE 0.23 → 0.46 m, fingerprint d28bb35c. Applied thrust trails commanded by ≈80 ms — motor lag, Fig. a. Error direction clusters at −70°: a mean gust from that heading, Fig. b. Altitude dips 0.11 m at take-off and recovers in 2 s: ≈ +9 % mass, Fig. c. We doubled the z-integrator clamp; the dip halved and the nominal column did not move.'

Four sentences: symptom, mechanism, evidence, intervention.

The instructor holds every team's true values and will not show them. A team whose mechanisms match the truth scores; so does a team whose mechanisms are wrong but whose argument was the right shape and whose numbers were honestly read.

Read the model answer aloud at the start of the debrief block; it visibly raises the standard of everything after it.

A note on honesty

Why peeking earns nothing

  • The values are hidden, not encrypted. team_plant("T3")._spec prints them. Nothing stops you.
  • Nothing rewards you either: the report is marked on the diagnosis of an unmodified controller — the plots, the argument, the one attributed change.
  • The instructor can re-run any submission with a different salt: same effect classes, new values. A controller that only works on the un-salted plant is its own confession.
The professional version of this rule. Flight-test engineers could often get the true parameters from the manufacturer. They measure anyway, because the skill being built — and paid for — is reading the aircraft from its data. The same is true in this room.
Say it once, lightly. Making it a big deal invites the behaviour.

Concept check · before the workshop

Three questions, then you fly

Q1 — Your realistic column shows a take-off sag but the nominal column shows none. A teammate says "so the mass offset must be huge." Is that inference sound?

No — any positive offset produces some sag; the size of the dip, not its existence, measures the offset. Initial acceleration is \( g(m/m_{\text{true}}-1) \). Read the depth and the recovery time; compare with the public +4…+14 % range.

Q2 — Commanded and applied thrust in log_r.f_cmd / log_r.f lie exactly on top of each other in your plot. What have you almost certainly done?

Plotted the nominal run — f_cmd is None there; on the realistic run they cannot coincide. The plant always lags; a zero gap is a bookkeeping error, not a finding.

Q3 — You raise kp_pos to fight the gust and the realistic track starts to oscillate. Which hidden effect just bit, and why did the nominal column not warn you?

GPS latency: the estimator corrects toward where the vehicle was; higher gain turns that delay into a limit cycle. The nominal model delivers fixes on time, so its phase margin is larger than the plant's — the Week-8 lesson, now with a number attached.

Q2 catches the commonest workshop error in the first ten minutes. Q3 is the bridge to the latency widget.

From the workshop to the project

What the gap proves, and what it does not

  • It proves that a number is a property of a controller and a plant, never of the controller alone. Your nominal RMSE was a demonstration on one plant.
  • It does not prove your controller is bad. It degraded gracefully on a plant it was never designed for; that is what a well-built classical loop does.
  • It leaves a question open: if one successful run is not evidence, what is?
Next week's answer, in one line. A written requirement with a threshold, a verification method anyone can re-run, and the evidence it produced — on the plant the requirement names. That chain is called certification, and it is the standard the final project is judged against.

The team plant retires today. The habit it taught — every number carries its conditions — is the one thing that carries forward.

Final project · kickoff

Act II: make it intelligent

The classical autopilot you just diagnosed is the baseline. The final project (40% of the course) upgrades it with Model Predictive Control — and proves the upgrade against a written specification. From here on the work is individual.

  • One mission for everyone: M3, the payload drop. No menu, no alternatives — same problem, same three requirements, same bars for all 35 of you.
  • One aircraft each. certify.py --student <your ID> builds your quadcopter — your mass, arm, torque ratio, inertia. Same problem, different vehicle. Next slide.
  • One method: MPC (taught Wk 12): constraints as first-class citizens; anticipation over reaction.
  • One verdict: certify.py — PASS or FAIL against fixed requirements, run the same way for every student.

The reality-gap workshop you just did stays a team assessment (20%). The final project — build, certify, defend — is yours alone, all 100 marks individual. Full brief + rubric: final-project-assessment.md; the spec itself: simulator/CERTIFICATION.md.

Final project · the brief

What you are being asked to do

The task. Take the classical autopilot you demonstrated today, run it on mission M3 against your own vehicle, watch it fail a requirement, upgrade it with MPC, and prove — against your own frozen baseline, under identical conditions — that the upgrade closed exactly that gap. You do this individually: your controller, your certification, your report, your viva.
  • Not 'implement MPC'. Anyone can run a library.
  • Not 'get the lowest RMSE'. A number without conditions is marketing.
  • Not a team deliverable any more. The reality-gap workshop was the team's; this one is yours.
  • The deliverable is an argument, supported by evidence, defended in person.

Every project defends one sentence: 'On mission M3, on my vehicle, my MPC controller improves [metric] from [baseline number] to [new number] under identical conditions, because [mechanism].' Everything else exists to make that sentence true and checkable.

Why it is framed this way. Because that sentence is what an engineer is paid to produce. Not code, not a plot — a defensible claim about a system, with the evidence attached. The rest of your career is that sentence, at varying scale.
The one-sentence claim structure is the spine of the whole final project; put it on the board and leave it there.

Final project · the new rule this year

Everyone gets a different aircraft

Your vehicle, from your student ID. Pass --student <your ID> and the harness builds your own quadcopter before it flies the mission: your mass, your arm length, your yaw-torque ratio \( k_\tau \), your inertia. Every one of them is derived from your ID, and the ID is required for the graded run.
# the graded command — the ID is not optional
python certify.py --controller <yours> --student <your ID> --mission M3

# see your own numbers before you design anything
python -m quadsim.student_params <your ID>

Deterministic, not random. The same ID gives the same aircraft on any machine, forever — so your result reproduces exactly. A different ID never gives your aircraft.

What this changes, and what it does not. Still allowed, and encouraged: discuss the theory, argue about horizons and cost weights, read each other's code, help each other debug an import error.

What no longer works: copying a tuned controller. A design that closes REQ-M3-1 on my vehicle is not guaranteed to close it on yours — the mass step, the authority and the margins are all different numbers.
Record your student ID in your README. A run with the wrong ID certifies an aircraft that is not yours, and it does not count.

This is also honest engineering practice: a controller is certified for an airframe, not in the abstract. Yours is now a specific aircraft with specific numbers, and you own them.

The single most important new idea in this kickoff — spend two full minutes. Run `python -m quadsim.student_params` live with a volunteer's ID, then with a second ID, so the room sees the numbers differ. Stress twice: collaboration on understanding is fine, copying a tuned controller simply does not transfer.

Final project · how you're actually tested

Certified, not demonstrated

Two different questions. "Does it work?" — you ran it once, it looked fine, you believe it.

"Is it certified?" — it was run against a written specification, by a procedure anyone can repeat, and every requirement has a recorded pass/fail with evidence.

Real flight software is never accepted on the first kind of answer. DO-178C (civil avionics), ISO 26262 (automotive) — different industries, same shape: a requirement, a verification method, and evidence. That chain is called traceability.

What this means for you.
  • No live flight demo. You submit your controller; certify.py runs it against the M3 spec, once, on estimator seed 0, on your own vehicle, the same procedure for every student.
  • The PASS/FAIL report is required evidence in your written report — pasted verbatim, not summarised.
  • Week 15 is individual vivas only — certification already produced the working-controller evidence before the session starts.
Run certify.py --controller <yours> --student <your ID> --mission M3 against your own baseline this week, before you build anything new — it costs nothing and it is the first entry in your evidence chain. Full spec: simulator/CERTIFICATION.md.
This is the term's framing for the whole project — spend real time on it. On the COURSE DEFAULT vehicle (no --student flag) the reference CascadePID does not certify on M3 (max = 0.540 m against a 0.50 m bar); the LinearMPC upgrade does (0.473 m). Say those two numbers aloud, and say plainly that they are the shared worked example — each student's own numbers differ. The next slide shows the reports.

Final project · the mission

M3 — the payload drop, specified exactly

The scenario. The quad holds position at \( [0,\,0,\,1.5] \). At \( t = 5\ \mathrm{s} \) its mass steps down — a package released (0.65 kg → 0.45 kg on the course default vehicle; your two masses come from your student ID). Your controller is never told. One continuous instance, no reset, no flag: it must notice the change and correct it through feedback alone, exactly as a real flight computer would. 15 s total.
IDRequirementBar
REQ-M3-1max deviation over the whole post-drop window< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-2max transient in the first 3 s after the drop< 0.50 m
REQ-M3-3position RMSE over the last 5 s< 0.50 m

Graded on estimator seed 0, on your vehicle. One command shape, same for everyone:
python certify.py --controller <yours> --student <your ID> --mission M3

Why a mass step, and why no menu. A step in a plant parameter is the one disturbance no fixed-gain loop handles gracefully — the hover thrust it learned is simply wrong afterwards. And one mission with fixed bars for the whole cohort means 35 results that can be compared, argued about and marked on the same scale — even though the aircraft underneath each one is different.
Stress 'never told'. Every year someone reads the mass from the sim and hard-codes the switch; that is not a controller, and the viva finds it in thirty seconds.

Final project · the baseline

Freezing a baseline, and why it is not bureaucracy

  1. Run your classical controller on M3 through certify.py, with --student <your ID>, seed 0. Keep the report.
  2. Record: your student ID, the requirement numbers, the seeds, the controller version (git hash), your aircraft parameters.
  3. Commit the certification script invocation and a baseline.json. Tag it.
  4. From Week 12, those numbers change only with instructor sign-off.
Seeds are part of the experiment. You are graded on seed 0, but the report needs one honest sentence on the spread from --seeds 0,1,2. On the default vehicle the reference CascadePID ranges 0.211–0.721 m across eight seeds on REQ-M3-1 — the verdict flips on the seed alone. The seed is not the grading rule; seed 0 is.
The failure this prevents. Without a freeze, the natural workflow is: implement the new method, find it is no better, quietly weaken the baseline, declare victory. Nobody does this dishonestly — it happens by drift, one small change at a time.

A frozen baseline makes your headline number mean something, and it is the first thing a viva will probe: 'when did you measure the baseline, and has it changed since?'

Freezing also protects you. If MPC genuinely wins, a frozen baseline is the proof; if it does not, an honest negative result against a fixed reference is a legitimate, gradeable outcome.

Framing the freeze as protection rather than restriction gets much better compliance.

Final project · the evidence

The report is three things, and at most six pages

  1. Both certification reports — baseline and submission — pasted verbatim. Not summarised, not retyped, not screenshotted into a table.
  2. What you changed, written as engineering — the MPC formulation: model, horizon \( N \), cost weights, constraints, and which are hard versus soft and why.
  3. One figure and one paragraph on the mechanism — the altitude trace through the mass drop, both controllers overlaid; the paragraph names the specific feature of MPC responsible for the difference.

Plus one honest sentence on the seed spread, and a paragraph on what you would do next.

What is not required. Constraint-activation plots, timing histograms, ablation tables — all optional stretch work. They are good engineering and they will be read, but a report without them can still score full marks.

Six pages is a ceiling, not a target. A tight four-page report that nails item 3 beats a padded six.
The figure that decides the grade. Item 3. If the two altitude traces are not overlaid on one axis through \( t = 5\ \mathrm{s} \), the reader cannot see what you did — and the accompanying paragraph has to say which feature of MPC did it, not that "MPC is better".
Say plainly that the optional items are optional. Last year's biggest waste of student effort was ablation tables nobody asked for while item 3 went missing.

Use the gates as designed. Each gate exists so that a problem surfaces while there is still time. Anyone who hides trouble until the Week-14 clinic has removed their own options. Bring your riskiest open question to the clinic, not your prettiest plot.
The fallback rule reduces panic-driven dishonesty; state it clearly and early.

Final project · what excellent looks like

Two anonymised examples, and why they scored

A certified project, high mark. Baseline: NOT CERTIFIED, REQ-M3-1 max 0.54 m on their own vehicle. Submission: CERTIFIED, 0.47 m. The overlaid altitude trace showed the baseline climbing 0.09 m before its integrator caught up; the MPC trace barely departs.

Their paragraph named the mechanism precisely: the horizon carries the altitude error forward, so the cost of the predicted climb is paid at the first sample after the drop rather than after the error has accumulated. One sentence, one figure, and the claim is closed.
An uncertified project, also a high mark. MPC came in at 0.51 m — still red, by 0.01 m. Rather than tuning until something passed, they reported the failure and diagnosed it: their horizon was shorter than the settling time of their vehicle's mass transient, so the predicted climb fell outside the window entirely.

They showed \( N \) versus max deviation, located the knee, and stated what they would run next. The four artifact items capped at 45/60 — and the viva scored in the top band.

Both share the same three things: a number predicted, then measured, a mechanism named rather than described, and honesty about what the evidence actually shows. Neither is about method sophistication.

Both are achievable in five weeks by one person who starts in Week 11 and uses the gates. Neither required unusual talent — they required not deferring the hard part.
Concrete exemplars set the standard far better than rubric language.

The report

Case-study report structure — eight pages, due Week 11

  1. Controller under test (1 p): which controller, which fixes, its nominal numbers; the git hash.
  2. The gap (1 p): the two-column table, the fingerprint, the track figure.
  3. Mechanisms (3 p): the centrepiece — three effects, each with symptom, mechanism, evidence plot, and a quantitative estimate against the public range.
  4. The one change (1 p): what, why, before/after on both plants, the nominal-vs-robust trade stated.

Where the marks concentrate. Sections 3 and 4 carry most of the weight. Section 2 is evidence, not achievement — a team with a large gap and three sharp mechanisms outscores a team with a small gap and a shrug.

What makes a run count

Reproducibility: the four conditions on every number

  1. The team id, exactly as registered — it is the plant. A typo is somebody else's aircraft.
  2. The fingerprint printed with the numbers, and the same one on every figure.
  3. The controller is unmodified between the two columns — the git hash is in the report.
  4. The seed is stated. --seed moves only the estimator noise; the plant does not move.
Why a number without conditions does not count. Because the claim being assessed is not "the aircraft flew". The claim is "our controller, unmodified, on plant d28bb35c, seed 0, produced this behaviour" — and only the four conditions support it.

It is the same standard the final project applies next week: a number without conditions attached is not evidence.

Teams may change the controller for the "one fix" — that is the point — but the change must be recorded and both columns re-measured. An undocumented change makes both runs unusable as evidence.

This slide connects the workshop to the scientific standard of the final project; it is the same rule in both places.

Before the workshop

Ninety seconds on what could go wrong, and why that is fine

  • Some teams' own controllers will not survive the team plant. That is normal and it is not a grading catastrophe — switch to cascade for the analysis and write down why yours did not.
  • The case-study grade weights analysis over achievement: a large gap with three sharp mechanisms outscores a small gap with a shrug.

What actually loses marks. Not a big gap. Not knowing why.

A team that can say "we sag 0.11 m at take-off, here is the plot, that is ≈ +9 % mass, here is what we changed and what it cost the nominal column" has demonstrated more understanding than a team whose controller happened to be robust.

Final project · the viva

What five minutes, hard cut, actually feels like

  1. 0:00–2:00 — explain your artefact. I pick a line from your certification report; you walk me through why it passed or failed and answer 'what would change it?'.
  2. 2:00–4:00 — depth probe. One question from the published bank — MPC formulation, the M3 mechanism, or the seed spread, at my discretion.
  3. 4:00–5:00 — code ownership. I point at a block of your submitted code and ask why it is there and what breaks without it.

The timer is a hard cut. It ends at 5:00 wherever we are — there is no presentation slot to make up the difference, so use the time you have precisely. And the questions are asked about your aircraft: your masses, your margins, your numbers.

What distinguishes a strong answer. Not fluency — mechanism. Compare:

'We used a soft constraint on tilt.' (describes)

'We softened tilt because a gust can put the state where no input satisfies a hard corridor, and an infeasible QP returns nothing — which on an aircraft means no command at all. Thrust stayed hard because physics enforces it anyway.' (explains)

Both are one sentence. Only one shows understanding.
You choose nothing: not the figure, not the questions, not the code block. That is what makes it an examination of understanding rather than of preparation — and why the published bank is safe to publish.
Demystifying the viva format reduces anxiety and improves preparation quality. Read both example answers aloud.

The one question I will ask everyone

The question. “What does your aircraft do badly, and how do you know?”
  • It is not a trick. Every controller is bad at something, and knowing what is the difference between having built a system and having got one working.
  • A good answer cites a measurement: 'it sags 11 cm at take-off on the team plant because it is about 9 % heavier than the controller believes — we measured it.'
  • A weak answer is a shrug, or a claim that nothing is wrong.
  • You will be asked it today in the debrief, and again in Week 15 about your final project.

Engineering is not the practice of making things that work. It is the practice of knowing, in numbers, the conditions under which they do — and saying so before someone else finds out.
